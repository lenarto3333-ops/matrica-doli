# 3. The Empress — Changing the Financial Pattern After 40

## In Brief

At around forty, this energy reaches a financial turning point: income that once depended on tirelessly—and often exhaustingly—giving oneself to work and caring for others must gradually develop into income grounded in the recognized value of accumulated experience and the ability to receive abundance as generously as one creates it. This is the shift from earning by “giving everything so everyone has enough” to earning because “I create value and allow myself to benefit from it fully”—and it determines whether the second half of life becomes more financially abundant or repeats the hidden exhaustion of the first.

## Natural Strength

By the age of forty, this energy has accumulated a unique resource—a deep practical ability to nurture a business, home, or relationship from modest beginnings into something lasting and abundant. What may seem like everyday care is actually capital in the form of patient mastery: knowing how to transform a small beginning into something valuable and enduring, consistently and step by step.

The natural strength of this turning point is the ability to transform accumulated experience in care and cultivation into a recognized, well-paid resource. What was once invisible daily labor can become a business after forty that produces a stable, generous income precisely because of its quality, proven over many years.

A second strength is the reputation of someone whose care and work produce tangible, reliable results. If quality and consistency have been maintained over the years, this reputation begins to work as capital after forty, attracting clients and partners who value material, tangible reliability rather than quick promises.

## Positive Expression

In the positive after forty, a person stops giving themselves without limit to work and caring for others and begins structuring income so that it returns generously to them as well. Earnings start to rest on the recognized quality of accumulated experience rather than on a need to prove usefulness repeatedly through self-sacrifice.

The positive financial pattern often shifts from exhaustingly providing for everyone around them to becoming someone who nurtures a mature, stable enterprise and takes genuine pleasure in its results. What was once invisible daily care becomes a recognized, properly paid asset.

The positive also brings an ability to monetize the creation of comfort, beauty, and care: through one's own business, consulting, or any work that requires patiently cultivating quality. People are willing to pay generously for what this energy does naturally—creating a tangible, warm result.

Another sign of a positive transition is the ability to enjoy the fruits of many years of work instead of immediately directing all income toward other people's needs, understanding that one's own peace and pleasure matter as much as caring for family.

### Positive Expression in Women

For a woman after forty, positive financial fulfillment often takes the form of moving from invisible, frequently undervalued care at home and at work into a recognized role where the quality of her work or care is valued and generously compensated.

She stops directing all income toward her family's needs while forgetting herself and begins allowing herself material comfort and pleasure as a deserved reward for years of invested work and care.

### Positive Expression in Men

For a man after forty, the positive financial pattern often shifts toward becoming someone who no longer merely provides the minimum but consciously builds stable, generous prosperity for his family and himself, drawing on years of proven, high-quality work.

He allows himself to invest not only in providing for others but also in his own comfort and enjoyment, understanding that this is not selfishness but a natural fruit of many years of conscientious work.

## Negative Expression

In the negative after forty, a person continues giving themselves to work and caring for others as exhaustingly as they did in their twenties and thirties, forgetting their own financial reward. This becomes increasingly draining because the physical reserves that once compensated for this strategy gradually diminish.

Financial anxiety in the negative is intensified by comparisons with people who seem to enjoy the fruits of their labor openly and generously. Instead of allowing themselves deserved comfort, the person continues postponing pleasure until some undefined “later” that never arrives.

Another negative expression is excessive attachment to accumulated property or savings as the sole source of security, without any real willingness to use them for personal comfort and joy.

In a deeper negative state, physical and emotional fatigue emerges from the role of “the one who provides for everyone.” If left unexamined, this fatigue can become hidden resentment toward loved ones for care that went unrecognized.

### Negative Expression in Women

For a woman, the negative state after forty often appears as a chronic habit of investing all income and care in the family while forgetting her own comfort and pleasure, to the point of feeling that her needs are “no longer important” or that it is “too late to think about them.”

### Negative Expression in Men

For a man, the negative state is often disguised as a duty to “always provide more,” while actually serving as a way to avoid acknowledging his own fatigue or need for support. He may deny himself deserved comfort even when the financial means are already available.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger at this age is comparing one's own level of comfort with that of people who, at first glance, freely and generously enjoy the fruits of their work—especially people who began with a similar experience of hard work.

A second trigger is the need to allow oneself to slow down and enjoy the results after forty, when the old strategy of “working and giving without stopping” is already exhausting the body, while permitting oneself to rest feels unfamiliar or even selfish.

A third trigger is a prolonged lack of recognition for accumulated experience and care from family members or clients who take generosity for granted without seeing how much labor lies behind it.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to conduct an honest inventory of the experience accumulated through creating quality and offering care over the years, listing the areas in which the person genuinely knows how to produce tangible, valuable results.

The second step is to reconsider the distribution of income consciously, assigning a regular share specifically to personal comfort and enjoyment rather than only to other people's needs.

The third step is to begin allowing oneself to slow down and rest gradually and at a safe pace, testing how years of accumulated experience continue to work even without constant self-denial.

The fourth step is to deliberately seek clients or an environment where quality and care are valued and gradually build income there through recognition rather than self-sacrifice.

The fifth step is to practice releasing resentment about years of unrecognized care that did not always convert into gratitude or financial gain: recognize that experience as a valuable foundation rather than an obligation that must continue forever.

## Main Task

The main task at this financial threshold is to move from earnings dependent on self-sacrifice and constantly providing for others to earnings grounded in recognized and generously compensated quality of care and work. This requires an inner reassessment: recognizing that the years of invested care were not an obligation without any right to reward, but became genuine capital in the form of experience—capital that after forty can and should finally be converted into stable, generous, comfortable prosperity for oneself as well as others.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I earn in the same way I did ten or twenty years ago, or has my approach evolved with me?
- How much does my current activity rely on the recognized quality of my care rather than unlimited self-sacrifice?
- Do I allow myself to enjoy the fruits of my work, or do I immediately direct everything toward other people's needs?
- What do I feel when I compare my level of comfort with that of other people my age and with similar experience?
- Am I clinging to the role of “the one who provides for everyone” even though it has long been exhausting me?
- Do I recognize the value of the years invested in care and work, even when they did not always convert into recognition or income?

## Practical Plan

1. Make a list of five areas in which you create the highest-quality and most valuable care or results for others.
2. Set aside a regular share of income for your own comfort and enjoyment, separate from spending on other people's needs.
3. Take one small step toward slowing down—a day off, a vacation, or an hour without work—even if it feels difficult at first.
4. Find clients or an environment where quality of care is valued and take the first step toward working with them.
5. Make a list of the years or projects invested in caring for others without adequate recognition, consciously thank yourself for the experience, and let go of the resentment.
6. Once a month, review your financial decisions and ask whether they were made from a habit of self-sacrifice or from a mature right to your own comfort.
