# 1. The Magician — Changing the Financial Pattern After 40

## In Brief

At around forty, this energy reaches a financial turning point: income that once depended on how quickly a person could seize a new opportunity must gradually develop into income grounded in an established reputation and a proven way of working. This is the shift from earning by “grabbing every new chance” to earning because “people know me and trust me”—and it is this shift that determines whether the second half of life becomes more financially stable or repeats the chaos of the first.

## Natural Strength

By the age of forty, this energy has accumulated a unique resource: a vast number of attempts, deals, connections, and ventures that may look like scattered activity but actually form a reserve of experience—an understanding of which offers work and which do not, which people can be trusted, and where caution is wise.

The natural strength of this turning point is the ability to transform all those accumulated attempts into a recognizable, reliable approach. What was once a series of separate, impulsive deals can become a systematic method after forty, valuable precisely because it has been tested over time rather than just invented.

A second strength is a broad network of contacts and a reputation as someone who knows how to negotiate and act. If agreements and promises have been honored with integrity rather than pursued solely for immediate gain, this network begins to work as capital after forty: recommendations, repeat business, and trust that cannot be bought or artificially accelerated.

## Positive Expression

In the positive after forty, a person stops indiscriminately chasing every new opportunity and begins to invest time selectively in areas where accumulated experience offers a genuine advantage. Earnings start to rest on confidence in one's own method rather than on a need to prove one's resourcefulness anew each time.

The positive financial pattern often shifts from constantly searching for new deals to creating a personal system or structure that produces income without requiring a daily personal presence: licensing an approach, teaching others, or building a business process. What was once a set of unrelated skills becomes an organized asset.

The positive also brings an ability to monetize the skill of teaching others how to act and negotiate quickly: mentoring, advising younger entrepreneurs, or teaching practical skills rooted in lived experience. People are willing to pay for a shorter path to results, and that becomes the competitive advantage of maturity.

Another sign of a positive transition is the ability to turn down deals that do not align with the reputation built over time, even when they appear profitable in the moment. The person understands their own value through years of proven work, not through a willingness to take on anything for quick returns.

### Positive Expression in Women

For a woman after forty, positive financial fulfillment often takes the form of moving from a constant search for new clients to building a recognizable name or brand that works on its own, supported by years of earned trust and a reputation as a reliable partner.

She stops proving her capability through every new deal and begins evaluating opportunities realistically, asking whether they align with her accumulated experience and long-term goals rather than focusing only on the desire not to miss a chance.

### Positive Expression in Men

For a man after forty, the positive financial pattern often shifts toward becoming someone whose judgment and approach carry authority within a particular circle. Younger entrepreneurs or colleagues turn to him for advice because they see lived, practically tested experience in him rather than theoretical guidance.

He allows himself to invest money in systems and processes that work over the long term rather than only in the next tempting deal, understanding that such investments create the stability that was missing during the first decades of active work.

## Negative Expression

In the negative after forty, a person continues chasing new opportunities just as they did in their twenties and thirties—grabbing every tempting offer and scattering their effort across ten directions at once. This becomes more exhausting because the reserve of youthful energy that once compensated for this strategy gradually diminishes.

Financial anxiety in the negative is intensified by comparisons with people who seem to have built a more stable income system. Instead of relying on accumulated experience and reputation, the person begins to doubt their value and searches for yet another quick opportunity where it would long ago have been wiser to invest in structure and consistency.

Another negative expression is the unwillingness to give up the habit of pursuing every opportunity, even when it is intuitively clear that this approach no longer brings enough stability or satisfaction.

In a deeper negative state, bitterness emerges from the sense that years of activity and countless deals have not translated into long-term financial stability. If left unexamined, this bitterness can turn into cynicism about one's own entrepreneurial ability.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger at this age is comparing one's own financial results with those of people who, at first glance, have built a more systematic and stable business or career—especially people who began with a similar level of experience.

A second trigger is the need to change one's habitual way of earning abruptly after forty, when the old strategy of “grabbing every chance” no longer works and a systematic approach feels slow and unfamiliar, particularly when financial obligations cannot easily be postponed.

A third trigger is the prolonged absence of recognition for accumulated experience from a younger generation or new partners who use different criteria for trust than those the person is accustomed to.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to conduct an honest inventory of the experience and connections accumulated over the years, listing what the person truly knows how to do better than others because of years of practice.

The second step is to reassess new offers consciously, separating decisions from the fear of missing an opportunity and tying them instead to whether a deal genuinely draws on the person's established reputation and is advantageous in the long term.

The third step is to begin systematically shaping accumulated experience into a method, product, or structure, even on a small scale, in order to test how years of trial and error can function as an independent, stable asset.

The fourth step is to deliberately seek an environment and market where mature, proven experience is valued—not only quick cleverness—and gradually build a reputation there through reliability rather than urgency.

The fifth step is to practice releasing bitterness about past years of chaotic activity that did not always convert into financial gain: recognize that experience as a valuable stage of learning rather than time wasted.

## Main Task

The main task at this financial threshold is to move from earnings dependent on a constant search for the next opportunity to earnings grounded in one's own mature reputation and proven method. This requires an inner reassessment: recognizing that the years of attempts, deals, and quick decisions were not pointless commotion, but became genuine capital in the form of experience—capital that after forty can and should finally be converted into stable, long-term financial returns.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I earn in the same way I did ten or twenty years ago, or has my approach evolved with me?
- How much does my current activity rely on my established reputation rather than a constant search for the next chance?
- Do I have a method or system I could share with or pass on to others instead of keeping it only within my personal practice?
- What do I feel when I compare my financial results with those of other people my age and with similar experience?
- Am I clinging to the old pattern of “grabbing every chance” out of fear, even though it no longer resonates with me?
- Do I recognize the value of the years invested in attempts and deals, even when they did not always convert into stable income?

## Practical Plan

1. Make a list of five things you can do better than others specifically because of your years of active practice.
2. Review your current offers and decline those that do not match the reputation you have built, despite their immediate appeal.
3. Begin one small project that systematizes your experience—a method, course, or consulting practice—even if it starts on a modest scale.
4. Find a community or environment where mature, proven experience is valued and take the first step toward participating in it.
5. Make a list of the years or projects invested in activity without an adequate long-term return, consciously thank yourself for the experience, and let go of the bitterness.
6. Once a month, review your financial decisions and ask whether they were made from fear of missing a chance or from mature confidence.
