# 6. The Lovers — Changing the Financial Pattern After 40

## In Brief

Around the age of forty, this energy reaches a financial threshold: income that once depended on how much a person was noticed, valued, or approved of must gradually evolve into income grounded in mature personal taste and accumulated experience. It is a transition from earning “to be liked” to earning “because I genuinely know how to do this”—and this very shift determines whether the second half of life becomes more financially stable or repeats the anxieties of the first.

## Natural Strength

Before forty, this energy accumulates a unique resource—a subtle feel for what people need and for the precise combination of aesthetics, care, and quality that creates trust. These are years of practice, experimentation, and mistakes in relationships and work. They often seem like ordinary life experience, but are actually the formation of capital built from taste and an understanding of people.

The natural strength of this threshold is the ability to turn accumulated sensitivity into a recognizable signature. What was once an intuitive response—“I like it this way”—can become, after forty, a conscious style, method, or approach whose value comes precisely from lived rather than learned experience.

Another strength is a wider circle of relationships and a reputation built over the preceding years. If relationships and collaborations were formed sincerely rather than strategically, this network begins to function as capital after forty: referrals, repeat business, and trust that cannot be bought or artificially accelerated.

## Positive Expression

In the positive, after forty a person ceases to depend on constant external confirmation of their value through work or income. Earnings begin to rest on confidence in their own taste and experience rather than the need to prove again and again that they are “good enough” for a client, employer, or partner.

In the positive, the financial pattern often shifts from carrying out other people's assignments to creating a personal product or service with an individual signature: an original style, a tailored approach, or consulting rooted in lived experience. Something once enjoyed “for the soul” can become a source of stable, respectable income.

The positive also brings the ability to monetize a talent for creating trust and beauty: mentoring, curating, and work in which not only the outcome but also the way it is achieved is valued. People are willing to pay more for the same product or service when it comes with warmth and taste, and this becomes a competitive advantage in mature adulthood.

Another sign of a positive transition is the ability to let go of financial habits formed from a fear of not being liked. The person stops underpricing their work for fear of losing a client or partner, recognizing their value through accumulated experience rather than through a constant willingness to yield.

### Positive Expression in Women

For a woman after forty, positive financial fulfillment often takes the form of moving from an executor's role into an author's role. She begins to create something under her own name—a brand, a practice, a creative product—drawing on years of experience working with people, beauty, and relationships.

She stops tying the price of her work to how approvingly others respond and begins assessing her contribution realistically, based on quality and years of practice rather than the desire to please a client or partner at any cost.

### Positive Expression in Men

For a man after forty, the positive financial pattern often shifts toward the role of mentor or someone whose judgment and taste become authoritative within a particular circle. Younger colleagues or partners seek his advice precisely because they recognize lived rather than theoretical experience in him.

He allows himself to invest money in what he truly values—quality, aesthetics, relationships—without feeling embarrassed before those who consider such spending unnecessary, because he understands that these investments create the long-term value of his work or enterprise.

## Negative Expression

In the negative, after forty a person continues earning as they did in their twenties and thirties: trying to please, adapting to the expectations of clients or employers, and lowering the price of their work out of fear of losing goodwill. This begins to drain them more deeply because the youthful energy that once compensated for this strategy gradually diminishes.

In the negative, financial anxiety is intensified by comparisons with people who appear to have achieved greater recognition or status. Instead of relying on accumulated experience, the person begins to doubt its value and looks outward for confirmation when it is long past time to trust themselves.

Another negative expression is reluctance to change an established but no longer effective financial pattern out of fear of losing stability, even when it is intuitively clear that the current path no longer resonates or brings enough money or satisfaction.

At a deeper negative level, bitterness emerges from the sense that years of sincere care for others—clients, colleagues, or a partner—have not translated into an adequate financial return. If left unexamined, this bitterness can turn into cynicism about one's own generosity.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger at this age is comparing one's financial results with those who appear to have achieved greater recognition or status, especially people with similar experience or talent.

Another trigger is the need to make a sharp change in one's usual way of earning after forty, when old patterns no longer work but new ones seem risky or unfamiliar, particularly when financial obligations cannot easily be deferred.

A third trigger is a prolonged lack of recognition for accumulated experience from a younger generation or new clients whose criteria for trust differ from those the person has grown accustomed to.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to conduct an honest inventory of the experience and taste accumulated over the years, listing what the person genuinely does better than others because of lived experience rather than learned theory.

The second step is to consciously review the pricing of one's work or services, detaching it from the fear of not being liked and tying it to the real value of accumulated experience.

The third step is to begin creating something under one's own name or authorship, even on a small scale, to see how accumulated sensitivity to beauty and relationships functions as an independent product rather than merely part of someone else's project.

The fourth step is to deliberately seek an environment and market where mature experience is valued, not just youthful energy, and gradually build a reputation there through quality rather than urgency.

The fifth step is to practice releasing bitterness about past years of generosity that did not always translate into financial gain: acknowledge that experience as a valuable stage of learning rather than time wasted.

## Main Task

The main task of this financial threshold is to move from earnings dependent on external approval to earnings grounded in one's mature value and accumulated experience. This requires an inner reassessment: recognizing that years of caring for others and being sensitive to beauty and relationships were not “soft” or secondary qualities, but became genuine capital that, after forty, can and should finally be converted into a worthy financial return.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I earn in the same way I did ten or twenty years ago, or has my approach evolved with me?
- How well does the price of my work reflect the true value of my accumulated experience?
- Is there something I could share under my own name rather than as part of someone else's project?
- What do I feel when I compare my financial results with those of other people my age and with similar experience?
- Am I holding on to an old financial pattern out of fear, even though it stopped resonating long ago?
- Do I acknowledge the value of the years I invested in caring for others, even when they did not always translate into money?

## Practical Plan

1. Make a list of five things you do better than others specifically because of your years of lived experience.
2. Review the current pricing of your work or services and raise it where it is clearly too low because of a fear of not being liked.
3. Begin one small project or initiative under your own name, even if its initial scale is modest.
4. Find a community or environment where mature experience is valued and take the first step toward participating in it.
5. List the years or projects invested in caring for others without an adequate financial return, consciously thank yourself for that experience, and release the bitterness.
6. Once a month, review your financial decisions to determine whether they were made from fear or mature confidence.
