# 12. The Hanged Man — Tasks from Past Incarnations

## Brief Essence

This position points to an experience in which a person was forced to stop—perhaps through illness, isolation, captivity, exile, or circumstances that deprived them of their usual freedom to act. It was likely during this time, when outward movement became impossible, that a deeper ability to see life differently emerged: something that could not be noticed while everything moved quickly. The task of this incarnation is to complete what began before by learning to choose the pause consciously as an instrument of understanding rather than experiencing it again as punishment or imposed inactivity. The past provides rare depth and patience, but leaves a habit of remaining in waiting even after every outside obstacle to action has disappeared.

## Natural Strength

From past experience, this energy brings a rare ability to find meaning and calm where others would see only lost control. It resembles a soul’s memory of a time when its own perspective was the sole remaining resource, which is why this person can find depth and understanding even in the most difficult and restrictive circumstances today.

Another inherited quality is a natural gift for seeing what is hidden behind the obvious. When direct action was impossible in past circumstances, attention likely shifted toward observation, analysis, and understanding concealed motives and connections. This underlies the present ability to read a situation more deeply than others.

A third strength is maintaining inner dignity where outside control has been taken away. Past experience likely taught this soul that even when circumstances cannot be changed, the freedom to choose one’s attitude remains. It left a rare inner resilience that now appears as composure in crisis.

A fourth quality is a deep, almost bodily understanding of the value of timing and readiness. Past experience in which waiting likely lasted longer than desired left heightened sensitivity to when action is truly due and when waiting remains wiser—a capacity that in this life can become either a gift for precise timing or the burden of chronic delay.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, past experience becomes mature wisdom about time. A person intuitively understands when to wait and when to act without hesitation, doing both without hidden fear or traumatic paralysis. They choose the pause consciously as a tool rather than the only available means of survival.

The inherited gift of seeing what is hidden unfolds as an ability to help others notice what they cannot see themselves—not from superiority, but from a sincere wish to share depth acquired at great cost. This person’s words and advice carry weight because they rest on lived rather than theoretical experience.

The inner resilience that once sustained imposed inactivity becomes an ability to remain calm in any circumstances—not from indifference, but from knowing that even the hardest period has a limit and meaning when lived consciously.

The positive also brings the ability to act in time without repeating the trauma of needless waiting. The person chooses movement consciously when it is possible instead of automatically remaining in the pause from old habit. This is not naïve haste, but a deliberate choice to trust the moment despite memories of a time when there was no choice.

Another sign is appreciation for the freedom to act available in this life, unlike the previous one. Past experience taught the person not to take the possibility of choosing for granted, and that gratitude now becomes active, conscious participation in their own destiny.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

In women, positive past-life experience appears as deep inner wisdom without bitterness. She naturally sees situations in full dimension, from several sides at once, but this capacity is no longer bound to the pain of enforced waiting. There may already have been enough periods of involuntary inactivity in the past for her to value consciously the freedom to act when she chooses in this life.

Her ability to remain calm in difficult circumstances is not an escape from feeling, but a sincere expression of tested resilience. She senses intuitively when patience is required and when a decisive step is due, and the decision arrives with remarkable confidence.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

In men, positively inherited experience appears as refined, calm leadership in crisis. He does not panic where others lose their footing, perhaps because past experience has already taught him that even the hardest circumstances leave room for inner choice.

He naturally holds a pause without anxiety, reads the situation more deeply than others, and acts in time without needless delay. This looks like rare composure, but is actually a well-developed capacity inherited from living through uncertainty.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, past experience appears as a hidden tendency to remain stuck, bordering on meaningless self-sacrifice. A person may subconsciously choose the role of the one who waits and endures even when direct action is fully available and nothing outside prevents movement.

Another negative expression is excessive dependence on the feeling that “it is not time yet” as a way to avoid risk. In this incarnation, it appears as an anxious habit of continually “waiting for a sign” instead of taking a step and seeing what follows.

The negative may also repeat the painful pattern of imposed inactivity. A person unconsciously creates or attracts circumstances in which they again feel confined, restricted, and deprived of freedom to act, as though the psyche were reenacting a familiar yet painful pattern.

At a deeper negative level, inner isolation persists even without outside barriers, as though the mind remembers a time when closeness or trust came at too high a price. It appears as keeping apart even when direct participation in life carries no real danger.

A fifth expression is sudden, chaotic action against a background of prolonged stagnation. Inner tension grows until the person lunges into action without preparation, as though sharp movement could compensate for years of inactivity.

### Expression in Women (Negative)

In women, the negative past-life expression often appears as chronic submission to circumstances, including those that can readily be changed. She may seek proof of her wisdom in how patiently she survives difficult periods instead of taking an active role in ending them.

In practical matters, this appears as fear of losing what little exists, causing her either to cling too long to outdated conditions or break them abruptly and without thought merely to feel some degree of control.

### Expression in Men (Negative)

In men, the negative expression often hides behind philosophical detachment—a defense against the fear of again entering a position with no choice. He may consciously avoid situations requiring direct responsibility, fearing that active participation will again lead to lost control.

The tendency toward prolonged stagnation appears as continually postponing important steps “until better times,” exhausting both him and those nearby as years that could have been lived actively pass in waiting.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger is any situation reminiscent of past lost control: a sudden restriction of freedom, dependence on another’s decision, or the sense that no exit is available. Even a minor echo of this pattern can activate disproportionately strong anxiety rooted in unresolved experience.

Another trigger is a prolonged lack of recognition for the inner depth and wisdom acquired through experience. Because the past developed value through seeing and understanding rather than acting, others’ failure to recognize it gradually undermines inner resources more deeply than may be obvious.

A third trigger is the need to act quickly without time for reflection. Past experience taught that hasty action in restricted circumstances could carry a high cost, so imposed urgency activates an especially strong desire to freeze.

A fourth trigger is contact with people or circumstances that resemble past isolation or enforced dependence in behavior or atmosphere, even when the person is not consciously aware of it. The response is often disproportionate because it arises not from the present, but from old experience.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

First, recognize that the present tendency to become stuck or avoid action may be rooted not in the current situation, but in an old experience of imposed inactivity. Simply admitting, “Perhaps this is not about now, but about then,” reduces the reaction’s intensity and opens space for a more conscious choice.

Second, deliberately practice small actions where stagnation once arose automatically. When you notice a recurring pattern of prolonged waiting without movement, stop and ask what actually keeps you in place: a real obstacle or an old habit.

Third, learn to feel the difference between an outside restriction that is real today and an inner restriction remaining from the past that no longer reflects your present freedom of choice.

Fourth, consciously value and use the freedom to act that this life provides. Each day, notice at least one opportunity that was unavailable in the past and use it deliberately, however modestly.

Fifth, gradually restore trust in closeness and participation where these may once have come at a great cost. Open in small steps to people and situations that carry no genuine danger this time.

Sixth, choose the pause consciously as a tool rather than the only available state. Gradually practice alternating mindful observation with concrete action instead of remaining trapped at either pole.

## Main Task

The central task of this position is to complete an old cycle in which pauses and restrictions were associated with having no choice, and to experience the ability to see more deeply as a gift rather than a memory of imposed inactivity. This means consciously alternating observation and action while fully trusting one’s freedom to choose—something that may not have been possible in the past.

When this task is fulfilled, inherited depth and patience cease to generate hidden anxiety and stagnation and become a true gift: a tool the person uses generously and freely when the wisdom of a pause is genuinely needed, without fearing that the pause will become a trap.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Is there a situation in my life where I have remained paused far longer than genuine reflection requires?
- What do I feel when I can either take a step or hide in waiting again?
- Can I recall a situation in which I chose to endure and wait even though direct action was fully available?
- How much is my tendency to observe from the sidelines connected with fear of losing control again?
- Are there people or situations that provoke disproportionately strong anxiety through a sense of restricted freedom?
- What happens inside me when I must act quickly without time for deep reflection?
- Do I trust myself enough to alternate pauses and action consciously instead of remaining trapped in one forever?

## Practical Plan

1. Notice one recurring situation of stagnation and describe it in writing from its first appearance to the present.
2. Each day, take one small, concrete action in a matter where habitual stagnation would previously have arisen.
3. Choose one opportunity unavailable in past experience and use it consciously this week, even on a small scale.
4. Before yielding to a strong desire to freeze and wait, ask: “Is this pause genuinely needed now, or is it an old habit from another time?”
5. Create one moment of conscious closeness or participation this week where you would once have preferred to remain apart.
6. Deliberately alternate: devote one day to observation and silence, and the next to concrete, visible forward movement.
7. Write a brief note of gratitude—even if it is never sent—to the past experience that taught depth of vision, while acknowledging that life now requires a different quality of presence.
