# 14. Temperance — Tasks from Past Incarnations

## Brief Essence

This position indicates an experience in which a person has lived a life marked by extremes—perhaps excess without measure or, conversely, a strict restriction and austerity that left no room for flexibility. Probably precisely because that experience was one-sided, to the very edge of one of two opposites, there arose in this life a deep, almost instinctive attraction to balance, moderation, and harmony. The task of this incarnation is to complete what has been done before: to learn to live consciously and calmly in the middle, and not to gravitate again to the same extreme from which, perhaps, it has not yet been possible to fully exit, either in the form of excess or in the form of excessive restraint. The past gives the rare gift of feeling the value of extremes, but leaves behind a habit of either fearing any certainty or, on the contrary, subconsciously reaching back to a familiar excess or limitation.

## Natural Strength

From past experience, this energy brings a rare wisdom about the price of lost measure — like a soul's memory of a time when excess or harshness led life to a dead end. That is why today this person has a keen, almost bodily sense of when something in life - his own behavior, relationships, affairs - begins to lean towards a dangerous extreme, long before it becomes obvious to others.

The second inherited trait is a natural gift for valuing gradualness and balance where others are chasing instant results. In past circumstances, when extremes were likely to be very expensive, a deep respect for slow, measured movement was formed, hence today's skill in building the steady, long-lasting instead of the fast but unstable.

The third strength is the ability to find beauty in the combination of opposites. Past experience must have taught this soul that pure, unrestrained extremes—whether excess or asceticism—lack the depth that is born only from the combination of the different—it left behind a rare aesthetic and spiritual flair for synthesis.

The fourth trait is a deep understanding of the value of restraint without suppression. A past experience in which either excess has led to loss or austerity has robbed life of joy has left a heightened sensitivity to the subtle difference between healthy measure and extreme, a capacity that in this life can be either a gift for a confident habitation of balance or a burden of chronic anxiety about any excess or limitation.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, past experience turns into a mature wisdom about measure: a person intuitively understands where the line is between healthy pleasure and excess, between discipline and self-restraint, and lives life with a rare inner balance, without fearing joy or depth of feeling. She knows how to consciously trust her own sense of proportion, and not perceive every pleasure or every limitation as a threat.

The gift of feeling the approach of extremes, inherited from the past, is revealed as the ability to help others find balance, not from a position of moralizing, but from a sincere desire to share the wisdom gained at a high price. The advice of such a person has weight, because it is based on lived, not theoretical, experience of extremes.

Respect for gradualness, which helped to survive in the former extremity, turns into the ability to build a stable, long-lasting life in any sphere - not because a person lacks the courage to make a sudden move, but because he already knows: true depth is not born from a rush, but from patient, measured development.

In addition, the ability to trust pleasure and satisfaction without repeating the trauma of loss of measure immediately appears: a person consciously allows himself to rejoice, create, love to the fullest, instead of automatic self-limitation from an old habit. This is not carelessness, but a conscious choice to trust one's own ability to maintain balance, despite the memory of times when this balance was lost.

Another sign is the ability to appreciate the harmony that this life has, unlike the previous one. Past experience has taught us not to take balance lightly, and in this life this wisdom translates into the ability to consciously maintain balance rather than taking it for granted.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

In a woman, past experience in the positive is manifested as a deep inner grace without fear of her own desires: she naturally feels where the healthy limit of pleasure or restraint passes, but this ability is no longer connected with the fear of punishment for excess or the malaise of excessive restriction. Apparently, there was already enough experience of extremes in the past, so that in this life she could consciously appreciate the opportunity to live in balance by her own choice, and not under the pressure of fear or prohibition.

Her ability to maintain balance in tempting or difficult circumstances is not an escape from feelings, but a sincere expression of experienced wisdom. She intuitively feels when the situation calls for restraint, and when for generosity and pleasure, and this decision comes with surprising ease for her.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

In a man, the inherited experience manifests itself as a refined, calm moderation in crisis and tempting situations - he does not rush into excess and does not lock himself into strict limitations, because, probably, past experience has already taught him that both extremes are equally expensive.

He naturally knows how to keep his balance during trials, see the situation more deeply than others and act in time, without forcing pleasure and without suppressing natural needs. This seems like a rare wisdom for him, which is actually a well-honed past skill of living in moderation.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, past experience manifests itself as a hidden urge to the same extreme, from which perhaps it has not yet been possible to get out: a person can subconsciously gravitate to excess - in food, spending, emotions, relationships - despite the awareness of harm, as if the old habit is stronger than the current reason.

Another manifestation of the negative is excessive, almost panic-stricken restraint as a tool to protect against the repetition of past extremes. In this embodiment, it manifests itself as a chronic denial of self-pleasure or joy, as if internally a person is still punished for that excess or that severity that he experienced earlier.

The negative can also look like a wavering between two extremes without a stable middle: a person sometimes plunges into excess, then sharply limits himself, punishing himself for the previous failure - and this cycle repeats itself again and again, preventing the establishment of a true balance.

At a deeper negative level, there is a tendency to internal condemnation - of oneself or others - for any deviation from a strict norm - as if the mind remembers a time when extremes were very expensive, and prefers to control every nuance so as not to repeat the same thing. This manifests itself as a habit of punishing oneself for the slightest deviation from the "ideal" balance.

The fifth manifestation of the negative is a chronic fear of any certainty, paradoxically hidden behind external moderation: a person fears that any clear choice is actually a hidden extreme, and unconsciously avoids even healthy, necessary decisions, so as not to risk repeating the past.

### Expression in Women (Negative)

In a woman, the negative manifestation of past experience often looks like a chronic distrust of one's own desire for pleasure - even when the external circumstances are quite safe. She may look for confirmation of her own "correctness" in how strictly she manages to limit herself, instead of allowing herself sincere, unforced joy.

In business, this manifests itself as a fear of allowing oneself the full realization of pleasure or success, which seems "too good to be true", which forces either to keep an inner distance from joy, or, on the contrary, to suddenly and thoughtlessly plunge into excess in order to finally feel at least some release from constant control.

### Expression in Men (Negative)

In a man, the negative manifestation is often disguised as a philosophical moderation in the face of temptations - a way to protect himself from the fear of reliving the extreme that once cost a lot. He may deliberately avoid a deep immersion in pleasure or passion, fearing that any intensity of feeling will again lead to a loss of control.

The tendency to a hidden urge to extremes in the negative manifests itself as unexpected, sharp breakdowns in excess just when the external restraint has lasted too long - as if an inner voice convinces that it is better to give in to yourself than to wait for an inevitable breakdown from the outside.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger is situations that remind of the past extreme: the temptation of excess or, on the contrary, the pressure of strict limitation, the feeling that one has to choose between "everything" and "nothing". Even the slightest hint of such a scenario can activate disproportionately strong anxiety, the root of which is an unlived experience.

The second trigger is long-term stability and moderation, which paradoxically causes internal tension, because past experience has taught us not to trust long-term balance as something too fragile. Because the past has built up readiness for a sudden breakdown in extremes, the very calm and balance can be felt as a harbinger of disruption.

The third trigger is the need to make a clear, uncompromising choice. Because past experience has taught us that any extreme can be very costly, the forced certainty at such times is especially strong in activating anxiety and the desire to avoid the decision altogether.

The fourth trigger is contact with people or circumstances that remind, either in behavior or atmosphere, of past excess or severe restriction, even if the person is not consciously aware of it. The reaction in such a contact is often disproportionate to the situation, because it comes not from the present moment, but from ancient experience.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

The first step is to realize that the current tendency to extremes or, on the contrary, excessive restraint may not be rooted in the current situation, but in an ancient experience of lost measure. A simple recognition that "maybe it's not about now, but about then" already reduces the intensity of the reaction and gives space for a more conscious choice.

The second step is to consciously practice moderation where previously the urge to go to extremes would automatically arise: if you notice a recurring scenario of excess or self-restriction, you should stop and ask yourself whether it is a real need or an old habit.

The third step is to learn to feel the difference between the healthy pleasure available today and the ancient fear of punishment for it, which remains from the past but no longer corresponds to present safety.

The fourth step is to consciously appreciate and allow yourself the balance that this life provides: to notice at least one moment of healthy balance every day that was unavailable in the past, and consciously allow yourself to trust it, even in small ways.

The fifth step is to restore confidence in one's own measure gradually, where in the past it probably came at a high cost: in small steps to allow oneself both pleasure and discipline, which this time do not carry a real threat of extremeness.

The sixth step is to consciously choose balance as a conscious choice, not as a way to avoid repeating the past, by doing it gradually: practicing alternating between conscious pleasure and concrete, conscious restraint, rather than getting stuck at one of the poles.

## Main Task

The main task of this position is to end the long-standing cycle in which extremes - excess or severe restriction - have been very expensive, and to learn to live the capacity for balance as a gift, not as a memory of forced or punitive measure. It means learning to consciously alternate between pleasure and restraint while maintaining complete confidence in one's own ability to maintain balance, something one may have failed to realize in past experience.

When this task is accomplished, the wisdom about the value of extremes and the ability to appreciate gradualness, inherited from the past, cease to be a source of hidden anxiety and wavering between poles and become a real gift - a tool that a person uses generously and freely in moments when the wisdom of moderation is really needed, no longer afraid that any pleasure or any decision will turn to a new extreme.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Is there a situation in my life in which I limited myself more strictly than is really necessary, out of fear of repeating the past extreme?
- What do I feel at the moment when I can either allow myself sincere pleasure or restrain myself again from an old habit?
- Can I recall a situation where I threw myself into excess because a long period of abstinence became unbearable?
- To what extent is my tendency to extremes connected with the fear of experiencing a loss of measure again?
- Are there situations or temptations that cause me disproportionately strong anxiety due to the feeling of impending breakdown?

## Practical Plan

1. To notice one situation of swinging between extremes, which is repeated, and to describe it in writing from the first manifestation to the present.
2. Once a day, make one small, concrete gesture of healthy moderation—allowing yourself a little pleasure or a little discipline where before there would have been extremes.
3. Choose one balance not available in past experience and consciously allow yourself to experience it this week, even on a small scale.
4. Before a strong desire to rush into excess or sharply limit yourself, ask yourself: "Is it really necessary now, or is it an ancient fear from another time?"
5. To create one moment of conscious, balanced enjoyment this week—where previously there would have been a desire for either excess or self-restraint.
6. Consciously practice alternation: devote one day to sincere pleasure, the other to conscious, calm discipline.
7. Write a short thank-you note—even without sending it to anyone—to an experience from the past that taught you to appreciate moderation, while recognizing that a different quality of trust in your own balance is now needed.
