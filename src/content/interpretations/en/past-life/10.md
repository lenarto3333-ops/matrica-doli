# 10. Wheel of Fortune — Tasks from Past Incarnations

## Brief Essence

This position points to an experience in which a person's fate depended heavily on forces beyond their control—perhaps the role of a traveler, merchant, sailor, or gambler whose life was determined more by the wind, the market, the cards, or a ruler's will than by personal plans. The soul likely experienced sharp turns of fate: sudden wealth and equally sudden loss, exile and an unexpected return, or a catastrophe after which everything had to begin again from nothing. The task of this incarnation is to complete what was begun before: preserve the flexibility and trust in life already gained, but learn to create one's own luck actively rather than merely adapting to someone else's turns of the wheel. The past brings rare adaptability and resilience in the face of change, but leaves behind a habit of feeling like a passenger in one's own destiny rather than its co-creator.

If past experience of change was mainly favorable—a series of fortunate turns, support from higher forces, or a lucky break that offered a way out—the present incarnation inherits an especially bright, easy trust in life. The task is then to remember one's own active role rather than to overcome fear of changeability.

## Natural Strength

From past experience, this energy brings a rare ability to adapt quickly to radically new circumstances: the capacity to begin life again where others would become lost and give up. This is not an acquired skill; it is like the soul's memory of times when survival directly depended on accepting a new turn and moving forward rather than clinging to what was lost.

A second inherited trait is an intuitive sense of the right moment: the ability to feel when to take a risk and when to wait. In past circumstances this was likely a matter of survival or prosperity—the merchant who knew when to sail or the gambler who sensed when to stop—and from it comes the natural sensitivity with which this soul still reads subtle signs of timing today.

A third strength is profound inner resilience in the face of loss. Past experience in which wealth and position could vanish overnight left an ability not to become too tightly attached to material things or status, with a deeper understanding that all of them are temporary and changeable.

A fourth trait is the capacity to believe in a second, third, or tenth life after a fall. Past experience probably taught this soul to rise after every blow of fate, leaving a heightened sensitivity to the very idea of fatality—an ability that in this life can become either a source of constant anxiety about another loss or, conversely, the gift of unbreakable faith that every fall is followed by a rise.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, past experience of changeable fate appears as wise flexibility: the person trusts the process of life while making active efforts where they have influence, understanding deeply that passivity and blind trust are not the same thing.

The adaptability of the past becomes a resource the person uses consciously: quickly readjusting after setbacks, seeing new opportunities where others become stuck in despair, and supporting people going through their own difficult turn of fate—the gift is applied deliberately rather than operating as a mere survival habit.

In the positive, a difficult past experience of sudden loss becomes a mature rather than anxious attitude toward material possessions and status. The person values what they have without clinging desperately to it because they know inwardly that true support lies not in things or position, but in their own ability to recover.

Another sign of the positive is the ability to trust new turns of fate, completing the old pattern of constant readiness for catastrophe through a new, calmer experience of accepting change as a natural part of life rather than a continual threat.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

In women, a past experience of changeable fate in the positive appears as calm resilience: she accepts turns of fate with dignity without losing her inner foundation, while actively participating in the creation of her own path rather than merely reacting to circumstances.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

In men, a past experience of risk or travel in the positive appears as calm, measured courage: he is not afraid of a new turn and does not act recklessly, trusting his own sense of timing sharpened through long experience.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, a past experience of sudden loss or dependence on someone else's will appears as chronic anxiety about change: the person continues expecting catastrophe even when circumstances are entirely stable, as though still living where control over their own life was unattainable.

### Expression in Women (Negative)

In women, this may appear as a deeply rooted habit of making no long-term plans because “everything can change anyway,” even to the point of abandoning personal ambitions in favor of constant readiness for the next turn that might destroy everything.

### Expression in Men (Negative)

In men, this may appear as a reckless dependence on risk disguised as courage, when it is actually an attempt to recreate the intensity of a past experience in which the stakes were always high.

If the past included the direct loss of everything through someone else's will or chance, in this life it may appear as an irrational fear of any stability, excessive caution in major decisions, or, conversely, a tendency continually to stake everything as though subconsciously testing whether the wheel will be kinder this time.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is a situation reminiscent of a past sudden loss: unexpected financial difficulty, a change of status, or loss of control over an important part of life.

A second trigger is a prolonged absence of movement or change, which conflicts with the deeply rooted need inherited from the past to check regularly that fate is still favorable and causes anxiety about stagnation even in stable, safe circumstances.

A third trigger is the sense that an outcome depends entirely on someone else's will or on chance, felt especially sharply by this soul, which is accustomed to the possibility of losing control of its life at any moment.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to acknowledge that past experience of a changeable fate taught valuable flexibility but does not require constant anxiety about the future in this life.

The second step is consciously to practice making long-term plans despite the fear that “everything can change,” reminding yourself each time that present circumstances provide more stability than the past experience did.

The third step, if there is an irrational fear of stability or, conversely, an attraction to unjustified risk, is to explore where it may have come from and gradually learn to trust the real, present capacity to influence your own life.

The fourth step is to return regularly to practices that combine acceptance with action: conscious planning followed by deliberate acceptance that not everything can be controlled.

The fifth step is to allow yourself to enjoy stable periods without constantly expecting a hidden threat, observing as calm rather than anxiety becomes a new, real experience.

## Main Task

The main task of this incarnation is to complete the cycle of dependence on external turns of fate begun in the past and learn to be an active co-creator of your own life rather than merely its passenger. Past experience of flexibility and resilience is a genuine gift worth preserving, but not at the price of constant anxiety. The true maturity of this energy lies in trusting the process while consciously creating desired turns through your own actions.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I feel that stability is dangerous even when there is no real evidence of danger?
- Do I make long-term plans, or do I always keep the possibility that “everything can change” at the ready?
- Where does my fear of losing control come from—the actual circumstances of this life or an older source?
- Can I distinguish a considered risk from an attempt to recreate the intensity of a past experience?
- What responds within me to the words “loss,” “instability,” and “new turn”—calm or anxiety?
- What flexibility from the past would I like to preserve, and what constant anxiety would I like to leave behind?

## Practical Plan

1. This week, make one long-term plan despite the familiar desire not to “tempt fate” or build far-reaching hopes.
2. Write down one situation in which irrational anxiety about change arose, and explore where it may have come from.
3. Practice conscious acceptance: identify what is currently beyond your control and deliberately release your anxiety about it.
4. Allow yourself one moment of complete calm in a stable situation this week without expecting a hidden threat.
5. If a pattern of unjustified risk or, conversely, excessive caution recurs, consciously interrupt it once this week and choose a measured rather than automatic response.
