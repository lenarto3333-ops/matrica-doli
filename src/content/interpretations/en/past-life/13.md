# 13. Death — Tasks from Past Incarnations

## Short summary

This statement refers to an experience in which a person has experienced an abrupt, perhaps painful, end to an important life stage—exile, breakup, business failure, loss of a position or role that defined their identity. Perhaps it was at the moment when the usual world collapsed that a deeper capacity for starting over was revealed, something that could not have been learned if things had continued as they had been. The task of this incarnation is to complete what has been done before: to learn to consciously and calmly live completion as a natural part of life, and not to meet each change again with panic or abruptness inherited from past experience. The past gives a rare courage for transformation, but leaves behind a habit of either being afraid to get attached or preemptively destroying everything, even before the real threat of completion has appeared.

## Natural force

From past experience, this energy brings a rare ability to survive and start over where others would have lost all hope. It's like the soul's memory of a time when the only way forward was to completely let go of the old life - and that's why today this person knows how to find strength and clarity even in the most difficult, most destructive circumstances.

A second inherited trait is the natural gift of sensing when a structure or system has exhausted itself from within, before it becomes apparent to others. In past circumstances, when a crash came suddenly, all attention was likely to be focused on the early signs of a break-up, hence today's knack for predicting the end where others are still unsuspecting.

The third strength is the ability to preserve inner dignity in a situation of complete loss. Past experience has probably taught this soul that even when everything external is taken away, what cannot be taken away remains, leaving behind a rare inner resilience that today manifests itself as a calm confidence even in moments of deepest crisis.

The fourth feature is a deep, almost bodily understanding that after the end always comes a beginning. Past experiences in which destruction eventually gave way to new life have left a heightened sensitivity to cycles of birth and ending, an ability that in this life can be either a gift for confidently inhabiting change or a burden of chronically anticipating catastrophe.

## Manifestation in the plus

On the plus side, past experience turns into mature wisdom about transformation: a person intuitively understands that completion is not an end, but a transition, and experiences even painful changes without hidden panic or traumatic destruction around them. She knows how to consciously trust the renewal process, and not perceive each change as a threat to survival.

The gift of foreseeing the end, inherited from the past, is revealed as the ability to help others prepare for the inevitable changes, not from a position of fear, but from a sincere desire to share the wisdom gained at a high price. The advice of such a person has weight, because it is based on lived, not theoretical, experience of transformation.

The inner stability that helped to survive the former destruction turns into a plus into the ability to maintain clarity in any crisis - not because a person does not care, but because he already knows: even the deepest collapse has a limit and leads to a new beginning, if it is experienced consciously.

In addition, the ability to trust attachment without repeating the trauma of losing everything at once appears as a plus: a person consciously allows himself to reinvest in relationships, affairs, identity, instead of automatically keeping his distance from an old habit. This is not naivety, but a conscious choice to trust life, despite the memory of times when trust was very expensive.

Another sign is the ability to appreciate the stability that this life has, unlike the previous one. Past experience has taught us not to take endings lightly, and in this life this wisdom turns into the ability to build something new consciously, without fear that it too will instantly fall apart.

### Manifestation in women is positive

In a woman, past experience in the positive manifests as a deep inner strength without bitterness: she naturally feels when it is time for change, but this ability is no longer associated with the pain of a forced collapse. Perhaps there had already been enough abrupt, violent endings in the past for her to consciously appreciate being able to end things by choice rather than under the pressure of disaster in this life.

Her ability to remain calm in crisis situations is not an escape from feelings, but a sincere expression of experienced resilience. She intuitively feels when a situation calls for conservation and when it calls for a decisive conclusion, and this decision comes with a confidence surprising for her.

### Manifestation in the husband is positive

In a man, the inherited experience manifests itself as a refined, calm leadership in crisis situations - he does not panic where others lose their footing, because, probably, past experience has already taught him that even after the deepest collapse there is a way to a new beginning.

He naturally knows how to keep his balance during transformations, see the situation more deeply than others and act on time, without delaying the inevitable and without forcing completion before time. This seems like a rare wisdom for him, which is actually a well-honed past skill for living with loss.

## Manifestation in the minus

In the negative, past experience manifests itself as a hidden tendency to destroy in advance, even before the real threat of completion has appeared: a person may subconsciously cut off relationships, affairs or plans, just so as not to experience a sudden, uncontrollable collapse again, as it already happened once.

Another manifestation of the negative is excessive anxiety before any attachment as a tool of protection against future loss. In this embodiment, it manifests itself as a tendency to keep a distance even in close relationships, as if internally preparing for an inevitable collapse, which in reality does not necessarily occur.

Minus can also look like a repetition of the same painful scenario of sudden loss: a person unconsciously creates or attracts circumstances in which he again finds himself in a position of collapse, betrayal or exile - as if the psyche reproduces a familiar, albeit painful, pattern.

In a deeper minus, there is a tendency to destructive behavior even when there are no external reasons for it, as if the mind remembers the time when everything was taken away at once, and prefers to do it on its own, in order to somehow control the inevitable. This manifests itself as a habit of cutting off good where it could develop further.

The fifth manifestation of the negative is a chronic fear of stability, paradoxically hidden behind external readiness for change: a person is afraid that any well-being is temporary, and unconsciously destroys it himself, so as not to wait for a worse blow from the outside.

### Manifestation in a woman is negative

In a woman, the negative manifestation of past experience often looks like a chronic distrust of the duration of anything good - even when the external circumstances are quite stable. She can seek confirmation of her own strength in how decisively she manages to break ties, instead of allowing herself to trust something for a long time.

In business, this manifests as a fear of putting full energy into a project or relationship that seems too good to be true, which forces one to either keep an inner distance or, on the contrary, to suddenly and thoughtlessly destroy everything in order to finally feel at least some control over the inevitable.

### Manifestation in the husband is negative

In a man, the negative manifestation is often disguised as a philosophical indifference to losses - a way to protect himself from the fear of experiencing again the sudden collapse of everything that has been built. He may deliberately avoid investing deeply in affairs or relationships, fearing that attachment will lead to another painful fall.

The tendency to preemptive destructive actions in the negative manifests itself as unexpected, abrupt decisions to break up stable relationships or affairs just when everything is going well - as if an inner voice convinces that it is better to end it yourself than to wait for the inevitable collapse from the outside.

## What can be negative

The strongest trigger is situations that remind of a past sudden loss: unexpected changes in circumstances, a feeling that the ground is slipping from under your feet, the collapse of something important without warning. Even the slightest hint of such a scenario can activate disproportionately strong anxiety, the root of which is an unlived experience.

The second trigger is long-term stability, which paradoxically causes internal tension, because past experience has taught us not to trust long-term prosperity. Because the past has built up readiness for sudden collapse, the very calm and quiet can feel like a delay before the storm.

The third trigger is the need to deeply attach to a person, cause or place. Because past experience has taught us that deep attachment can be very costly in the event of a sudden loss, forced closeness at such times activates anxiety and the desire to keep a distance especially strongly.

The fourth trigger is contact with people or circumstances that remind, either in behavior or atmosphere, of a past breakdown or exile, even if the person is not consciously aware of it. The reaction in such a contact is often disproportionate to the situation, because it comes not from the present moment, but from ancient experience.

## How to return energy to the plus side

The first step is to realize that the current tendency to destroy in advance or to avoid attachment may not be rooted in the present situation, but in a long-ago experience of sudden loss. A simple recognition that "maybe it's not about now, but about then" already reduces the intensity of the reaction and gives space for a more conscious choice.

The second step is to consciously practice trust where previously there would be an automatic desire to keep a distance: if you notice a recurring scenario of the destruction of stable relationships or affairs, you should stop and ask yourself what is driving this - a real threat or an old habit.

The third step is to learn to feel the difference between the external instability that is real today and the internal expectation of collapse that is left over from the past but no longer corresponds to present security.

The fourth step is to consciously appreciate and allow yourself the stability that this life provides: to notice at least one well-being that was unavailable in the past every day, and consciously allow yourself to trust it, even in small things.

The fifth step is to restore trust in attachment gradually, where in the past it probably came at a high cost: to open up in small steps to people and projects that this time do not carry a real threat of sudden collapse.

The sixth step is to consciously choose ending as a conscious choice, not as a way of preempting catastrophe, by doing it gradually: practicing alternating between conscious holding and concrete, conscious letting go, rather than getting stuck in one of the poles.

## The main task

The main task of this position is to complete the long-standing cycle in which transformation and ending were associated with violent, sudden loss, and to learn to live the capacity for change as a gift, not as a memory of forced collapse. It means learning to consciously alternate between trust and letting go, while maintaining full confidence in your own ability to survive any ending, something you probably failed to realize in past experiences.

When this task is accomplished, the courage of transformation and the ability to begin anew, inherited from the past, cease to be a source of hidden anxiety and destructiveness and become a real gift - a tool that one uses generously and freely in moments when the wisdom of ending is really needed, no longer afraid that each change will turn into a disaster.

## Questions for self-reflection

- Is there a situation in my life in which I destroyed something stable even before the real threat of completion appeared?
- What do I feel at the moment when I can either allow myself to trust deeply, or keep my distance again?
- Can I recall a situation where I broke off a good thing because I was afraid of losing it later and suddenly?
- How much of my tendency to keep my distance is related to the fear of experiencing a sudden collapse again?
- Are there people or situations that cause me disproportionately strong anxiety due to the sense of impending disaster?

## Practical plan

1. To notice one situation of destruction in advance that is repeated, and to describe it in writing from the first manifestation to the present day.
2. Once a day, make one small, concrete gesture of trust in a matter where before there was a desire to stay away.
3. Choose one stability not available in past experience and consciously allow yourself to experience it this week, even on a small scale.
4. Before a strong desire to suddenly destroy everything, ask yourself: "Does this really need to end now, or is it an ancient fear from another time?"
5. Create one moment of conscious, deep attachment this week — where you would have wanted to stay away before.
6. Consciously practice alternating: devote one day to strengthening the stable, the other to the conscious, calm completion of what has really run out.
7. Write a short thank-you note—even without sending it to anyone—to an experience from the past that taught you the courage to transform, while recognizing that a different quality of trust in life is needed now.
