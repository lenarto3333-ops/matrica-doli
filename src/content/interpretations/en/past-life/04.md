# 4. The Emperor — Tasks from Past Incarnations

## In Brief

This position points to experience connected with power and responsibility—perhaps a life in which the person governed others and built something large, or, conversely, suffered under another's harsh authority and had no choice of their own. The task of this incarnation is to complete what was begun before: to carry structure and responsibility without turning them into either tyranny over others or constant fear of all authority. The past grants a strong innate sense of order and an ability to build for the long term, but leaves behind wariness of control—personal or external—that should be understood rather than blindly repeated.

## Natural Strength

The natural strength of this position is a deeply ingrained ability to organize complexity into simplicity. A soul already experienced in managing a family, community, or enterprise brings skills that appear almost without training in this life: seeing structure where others see chaos and making decisions quickly and confidently where others hesitate.

Another trait from past experience is a sense of natural dignity in the role of being responsible for others. This does not always appear as a desire for formal authority, but more often as an inner readiness to make a difficult decision when circumstances demand it, without wondering whether one has the right.

Natural strength also appears as resilience under pressure. A soul that has already carried the weight of responsibility is less frightened by difficult circumstances in this life. Where others panic, this person instinctively searches for a plan of action—a skill developed long before this incarnation.

Finally, past experience leaves a deep appreciation for legacy—what is built not for an immediate result, but for those who come later. This appears as a natural desire to create something lasting: an enterprise, home, tradition, or reputation, and care for it as though it were larger than one lifetime.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, this past appears as a mature, conscious ability to exercise power responsibly. The person uses developed organizational and managerial skills to create stability around themselves—in family, work, or community—without constantly needing to prove their right to the role.

Past experience of governing or leadership becomes wisdom about the limits of power. The person intuitively senses where healthy leadership ends and suppression of another's will begins, and consciously does not cross that line even when able to do so.

In relationships, the positive expression gives rare generosity of authority: the person shares accumulated experience and structure without fearing that it will weaken their position. They gladly teach others what they once mastered, understanding the value of transmitting knowledge rather than withholding it.

Another sign of the positive state is the ability to acknowledge past abuses of power without excessive self-blame. If the soul's deeper experience included misuse of control, this incarnation expresses a conscious, calm commitment not to repeat the pattern, without paralyzing guilt.

The positive is also visible in how the person relates to authority held by others: they respectfully recognize authority when it is earned and do not constantly test or challenge another person's competence from an old habit of trusting no one but themselves.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

For a woman, this experience in the positive often appears as a natural ability to lead a family, enterprise, or community without constantly proving her right to the role to people who may expect different behavior from her. She carries responsibility calmly and without drama, grounded in a deeply rooted sense of competence.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

For a man, this experience in the positive often appears as mature, effortless confidence in the role of organizer and decision-maker. He uses past managerial experience to support others rather than continually asserting his superiority.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, past experience of power—or subjection to another's harsh power—appears as extremes in the relationship with control. One extreme is excessive authoritarianism: the person repeats a pattern of unilateral rule, struggles to share authority, and treats any objection as a threat to status without understanding the source of such an intense reaction.

The other extreme is a deep, almost irrational fear of all power or structure. The person avoids leadership roles despite having a gift for them because, at a deeper level, power is associated with danger, oppression, or abuse experienced in the past.

Another negative expression is repetition of the fall-from-power scenario. The person unconsciously creates situations in which they lose control, status, or what they built, as though inwardly expecting every position of strength to eventually be taken away.

In relationships, the negative often appears as distrust of other people's intentions around power. The person suspects leaders, partners, or even loved ones of secretly wanting to control them when there is no real basis for it.

Attachment to control also appears as an inability to acknowledge defeat or the end of a period of influence without experiencing catastrophe, as though losing any position of power called into question the person's very right to exist.

### Expression in Women in the Negative

For a woman, the negative often appears either as excessive rigidity as a leader or family matriarch, or as chronic refusal of every responsible position because of an unspoken fear of repeating someone else's—or her own—past abuse of power.

### Expression in Men in the Negative

For a man, the negative often appears as a rigid, inflexible model of leadership inherited from deeper experience in which power was associated solely with force and control, with no recognition of dialogue or compromise.

## What Can Pull This Energy into the Negative

The most common trigger is being deprived of deserved influence or recognition: unjust removal from leadership, having one's opinion ignored, or accumulated experience devalued. This activates a deep memory of losing a position that once mattered.

A second trigger is encountering blatant abuse of another's authority, resonating with painful past experience and provoking a disproportionate reaction—rage, panic, or, conversely, submission that does not match the present situation.

A third trigger is being required to assume responsibility without corresponding authority or resources, recreating the sense of powerless authority—being nominally accountable without real influence.

A fourth trigger is prolonged exposure to a chaotic, unpredictable environment without structure, one of this soul's hardest trials because it contradicts its deep need for order.

Finally, isolation in responsibility pulls this energy into the negative: repeatedly becoming the only person who “holds everything together” without allies or support recreates an old pattern of solitary, exhausting power.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to recognize that present life does not have to repeat the past. When holding power or influence now, the person can consciously choose a different way of using it.

The second step is to practice shared rather than unilateral responsibility: consciously seek allies, co-leaders, and partners in matters where the entire burden was previously taken on alone.

The third step is to examine reactions to other people's authority without automatic distrust, checking each time whether suspicion has a real basis or is an echo of old experience projected onto the present.

The fourth step is to learn to release leadership and influence in time without feeling that identity is threatened. A useful practice is to deliberately prepare a successor or transfer part of one's authority before necessity demands it.

The fifth step is to work with physical tension connected to old experience of power or submission. Grounding, breathing exercises, or body-based therapy can help release an outdated readiness for constant defense or control.

The sixth step is to consciously recognize personal contribution and competence without excessive modesty or a constant need to prove them, finding a calm middle ground.

The seventh step is to practice trusting structures built by others: delegate not only tasks, but also the right to make decisions, observing that this does not necessarily lead to chaos or loss.

## Main Task

The main task of this position is to integrate past experience of power so that it serves growth rather than repeating patterns of tyranny or fear of control. This means recognizing that power is neither inherently good nor inherently threatening; it becomes one or the other depending on the inner state from which it is used.

When this task is fulfilled, the person can freely accept responsibility when appropriate and release it just as freely when the time comes, without attachment to status or fear of losing it. The natural capacity to build and organize does not disappear, but is cleansed of its ancient burden and becomes an instrument of service rather than protection against once-experienced helplessness or pain.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Which reactions to authority—my own or someone else's—seem stronger than the situation warrants?
- Can I recall a time when I shared power or control and it proved to be the right decision?
- How do I react to the thought of losing influence or status?
- How easily can I trust other people's decisions when I am formally responsible for the result?
- What distinguishes healthy leadership from control driven by fear for me?
- Am I preparing people who could take up responsibility after me?
- When did I last acknowledge my own competence aloud without minimizing or overemphasizing it?

## Practical Plan

1. For one week, observe your reactions to other people's authority and note when they seem disproportionately strong.
2. Find one matter where responsibility can be consciously shared with a partner or colleague instead of decided alone.
3. Use a brief grounding practice when tension around control or responsibility appears.
4. Begin preparing a “successor” in one area by transferring knowledge or authority to someone capable of receiving it.
5. Revisit a past situation where loss of position or influence still hurts, and consciously acknowledge that it is over.
6. Once this week, entrust an important decision to another person and do not interfere in its execution.
7. Write down three achievements from your role as a responsible person and read the list aloud to yourself.
