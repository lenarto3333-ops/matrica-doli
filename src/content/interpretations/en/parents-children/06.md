# 6. The Lovers — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In family bonds, this energy values warmth and sincere connection above all—not the formal performance of being a “good parent” or an “obedient child,” but a living, emotionally rich bond. Whether expressed as a parent or a child, this energy flourishes where there is room for tenderness without excessive control and for individuality without fear of losing love through disagreement.

## Natural Strength

The natural resource of this energy in the role of a parent is the ability to create an atmosphere of warmth and acceptance at home, where a child feels wanted as they are rather than as they are “supposed to be.” It appears in small things: the tone of voice, celebrating modest victories, and taking a genuine interest in what the child enjoys even when it differs from parental expectations.

Another strength is the ability to pass on a sense of beauty and aesthetics through example rather than instruction: cooking together, arranging the home, and paying attention to life's small pleasures. Children raised in this atmosphere often carry away from childhood not a set of specific rules, but the feeling that life can be warm and beautiful.

In the role of a child, the natural strength of this energy is an ability to sense the subtlest shades in a parent's mood, understand when things are hard for them, and instinctively look for ways to bring joy or harmony into the home, even during difficult periods in family life.

## Positive Expression

As parents, this energy in the positive raises children through acceptance rather than control: the child is allowed to have personal tastes, interests, and mistakes, which are regarded not as threats or disappointments but as a natural part of growing into themselves. Parents in the positive delight in their child's individuality even when the child is unlike them.

Communication with children is open and warm: difficult subjects are not silenced but discussed with respect for the child's age, without excessive drama or, conversely, dismissal of the child's feelings. The child feels able to bring any question or problem without fearing judgment.

As an adult child relating to one's own parents, this energy in the positive can see them realistically—with their strengths and weaknesses—and build a relationship on this mature acceptance rather than a child's expectation of perfection or constant approval.

Another sign of the positive state is the ability to let children enter independent life without feeling that one's purpose has been lost. Parents with this energy can celebrate their children's growing independence, maintaining a warm connection without keeping them artificially close to satisfy their own need for their presence.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

As a mother, a woman with this energy in the positive creates a home filled with warmth and beauty without turning it into a staged “perfect picture” for others. Her care for her children comes from genuine desire, not from a need to prove to herself or those around her that she is a “good mother.”

She knows how to support her children in difficult moments without losing her own identity beyond motherhood, showing them the example of a woman who loves and cares while remaining a whole person in her own right.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

As a father, a man with this energy in the positive is not ashamed to show tenderness and emotional closeness to his children, breaking the stereotype of the restrained, distant father. He takes an active part in creating a warm atmosphere at home instead of leaving it entirely to his partner.

He teaches children to value beauty and care for others through his own example rather than lectures, and he can acknowledge his mistakes to them, showing that closeness does not require perfection.

## Negative Expression

As parents, care in the negative turns into excessive control driven by anxiety: fear that something will happen to the child or that they will make the “wrong” choice leads to overly frequent intervention, limiting the child's natural independence under the guise of love and concern.

Another negative expression is conditional love, even when it is never stated outright. The child senses that parental approval and warmth depend on achievements, behavior, or meeting expectations, and this creates an anxious dependence on external validation in adult life.

In the role of an adult child, the negative appears as difficulty letting go of an idealized or, conversely, excessively critical image of one's parents. A person may continue seeking the affirmation and approval from aging parents that they never received in childhood, or hold on to an old resentment that now prevents a mature, warm relationship with them.

At a deeper negative level, adult children may become overprotective toward elderly parents, with care turning into control because of the adult child's anxiety rather than the parents' actual needs. This can provoke resistance and tension within the family.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger for parents is a situation in which a child chooses a path radically different from parental expectations or ideas about a “proper” life. This activates anxiety about the child's future and risks growing into excessive control.

Another trigger is outside criticism of one's parenting or maternal qualities—from relatives, society, or even the child. This energy experiences such criticism particularly sharply because it touches a deep need to be a “good enough” parent.

A third trigger for an adult child is contact with parents at moments when old childhood patterns become active: the feeling that approval must be earned or, conversely, that one must defend against long-standing criticism, even if the relationship has objectively changed.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step for parents is to consciously distinguish care from anxious control. Before intervening in a child's choice, ask yourself whether there is a real danger or whether this is your own fear of losing control of the situation.

The second step is to express love and acceptance to children regularly without tying it to achievements or behavior, through words or gestures that are not connected to an assessment or praise for a specific result.

The third step for an adult child is to consciously reconsider their image of their parents, seeing them realistically, with their own limitations and the context in which they raised their children. This does not mean excusing pain they may have caused, but it does mean no longer remaining forever in the position of the wounded child.

The fourth step is to practice releasing control in both directions within family relationships: parents learn to trust their adult children with their own path, while adult children acknowledge elderly parents' right to make their own decisions as long as their safety is not at risk.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to learn to love without conditions or excessive control while, from your own adult position, letting go of the need for constant validation from your parents. This concerns both roles at once: being the parent who gives a child a safe space for individuality and being the adult child who is no longer emotionally dependent on the approval or criticism of their own parents.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Does my care for my children arise from their actual needs or from my own anxiety?
- How much does my love for my children depend on their behavior or achievements?
- How do I feel when I think of my parents—with warmth, resentment, or a mixture of both?
- Am I still seeking affirmation or approval from my parents, even as an adult?
- Do I give children—my own or those I imagine having in the future—room to make choices different from mine?
- Can I see my parents realistically, with their limitations and the context of their lives?

## Practical Plan

1. Once this week, express love and acceptance to a child—or to yourself as your inner child—without tying it to any achievement or behavior.
2. Before next intervening in a child or loved one's choice, ask yourself: “Is this about a real danger or my own fear?”
3. Write a short letter to your parents—you do not have to send it—in which you honestly and calmly describe both the gratitude and pain connected with your relationship.
4. Notice one moment when an old childhood pattern is activated in contact with your parents, and consciously pause before reacting.
5. Create one shared ritual of warmth with a child or close family member: an activity, a conversation, or a walk without any specific goal.
6. Revisit one childhood situation from an adult perspective, with a realistic understanding of the context in which your parents acted.
