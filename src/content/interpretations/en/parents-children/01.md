# 1. The Magician — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In family relationships, this energy values lively, active connection above all—not silent coexistence under the same roof, but a space where people can experiment, ask questions, take initiative, and receive honest answers. Whether expressed as a parent or as a child, this energy thrives where there is freedom to act independently and trust that a promise made within the family will be kept.

## Natural Strength

The natural resource of this energy in the role of a mother or father is the ability to encourage independence and resourcefulness in a child, giving them room to try new things, make mistakes, and find their own solutions instead of doing everything for them. It shows up in small ways: a willingness to answer endless “why” questions and an openness to children's ideas, even when they seem unrealistic.

Another strength is the ability to speak with children as equals in a conversation—explaining difficult things in simple language and sparking their interest instead of merely giving orders. Children raised in this atmosphere often carry forward not a fear of making mistakes, but a habit of trying and asking questions when something is unclear.

In the role of a child, the natural strength of this energy is a lively mind and quick reactions: the ability to find common ground with parents even through difficult periods, ease tension with a timely comment or joke, and naturally seek compromise where other family members see a dead end.

## Expression in the Positive

In its positive expression, this energy approaches parenting by encouraging initiative: children are allowed to try new things, ask questions, and take an age-appropriate part in decisions that affect them. Parents in the positive take pleasure in their child's resourcefulness and independence, even when the child makes choices different from their own.

Communication with children is lively and honest: difficult subjects are not avoided but discussed directly, with respect for the child's level of understanding. The child feels able to come forward with any question and receive a truthful answer rather than an evasive one.

When expressed by an adult child in relation to their own parents, this energy supports a lively dialogue between equals, even when their views differ, and finds ways to discuss disagreements without breaking contact.

Another sign of the positive state is the ability to keep one's word to a child: promises are fulfilled rather than forgotten amid other responsibilities. The child grows up feeling that their parents' word can be trusted, which in turn develops their own capacity to trust others and honor commitments later in life.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

As a mother, a woman expressing this energy positively creates an atmosphere of lively dialogue and encouraged initiative at home, without turning parenting into constant control. She takes a genuine interest in her child's ideas and plans and supports their independent efforts, even when it would be easier to do something for them.

She knows how to be a source of support for her children in difficult moments while remaining active in her own life, showing them the example of a woman who has retained her appetite for new experiences despite the demands of motherhood.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

As a father, a man expressing this energy positively takes an active part in shared activities and adventures with his children rather than staying on the sidelines of their upbringing. He answers their questions honestly and fully, showing that curiosity about the world is natural and important at every age.

He teaches independence through his own example rather than through lectures, and he can admit his mistakes to his children, demonstrating that taking action matters more than always appearing to be right.

## Expression in the Negative

In its negative expression, parental activity turns into inconsistency: there is one set of rules today and a completely different one tomorrow; promises are made easily and forgotten just as easily amid new pursuits. A child in this atmosphere does not always know what to expect, undermining their basic sense of stability.

Another negative expression is using persuasiveness and wit as tools of control. Instead of honestly explaining the reason for a restriction or demand, a parent talks circles around the child, distracts them, or uses clever tactics in place of a direct conversation. Gradually, the child learns not to take adults' words at face value.

For an adult child, the negative expression appears as difficulty trusting a parent's promises because of their inconsistency during childhood. The person may either continue to place hope in promises that were rarely kept or, conversely, become excessively suspicious of any assurance, even a completely sincere one.

At a deeper negative level, the pattern of inconsistency may be repeated with one's own children or while caring for aging parents: promises and good intentions are present, but repeatedly failing to follow through gradually erodes the whole family's trust.

## What Can Pull This Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger for parents is a prolonged, monotonous domestic routine that leaves no room for their own activity. The resulting inner boredom may be compensated for through inconsistency in rules or promises made to children.

A second trigger is the pressure to remain a “perfect” role model for the child at all times. This can provoke excessive control driven by anxiety or, conversely, avoidance of difficult conversations out of fear of not finding the right words.

A third trigger for an adult child is contact with parents at moments when old disappointments over broken childhood promises are reactivated, even if the relationship has objectively changed and the parents have become more reliable.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step for parents is to consciously limit promises to those they can realistically keep, then follow through consistently—even in small matters.

The second step is to explain the reasons behind decisions or restrictions directly and regularly rather than using clever persuasion or distraction, even when an honest answer is more difficult or less convenient in the moment.

The third step for an adult child is to consciously revise their image of their parents: to acknowledge both the genuine inconsistency that caused pain and the context in which their parents acted, without chronically expecting that this time a promise will certainly be kept without confirmation.

The fourth step is to practice consistency in small, everyday family interactions. If something has been promised, write it down or set a reminder so it gets done instead of relying solely on memory amid other responsibilities.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to learn to combine lively initiative and encouragement of independence with the reliability of one's word, giving a child or adult relative a sense of stability. This applies to both roles at once: to be a parent whose word carries weight precisely because it is always supported by action, and to be an adult child who learns either to trust again or to release old resentment over broken promises from the past.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I keep the promises I make to children or close relatives, even when other responsibilities arise?
- How consistent are my rules and expectations for a child from one day to the next?
- How do I feel when I think about the promises my parents made to me in childhood?
- Do I still trust my parents' word, or do I wait for proof before I believe them?
- Do I give children—my own, or those I imagine having in the future—room to try things independently and make mistakes?
- Do I explain the reasons behind decisions directly, or do I more often distract and persuade through clever tactics?

## Practical Plan

1. Make a list of the promises you have made to a child or close family member over the past month and check how many you fulfilled.
2. Before making your next promise to a child, consciously assess whether you can realistically keep it.
3. Write a short letter to your parents—you do not have to send it—calmly and honestly expressing both gratitude and disappointment over broken promises from childhood.
4. Explain the full reason for one decision or restriction to a child instead of falling back on “because I said so.”
5. Notice one moment when an old disappointment about a broken promise is activated in contact with your parents, and consciously pause before reacting.
6. Create one shared activity ritual with a child or close family member—a new game, an outing, or a joint project—and see it through to completion.
