# 11. Strength — Relationships with Parents and Children

## Brief Essence

In relationships between parents and children, eleventh energy appears through endurance, reliability, and quiet inner strength that can easily be confused with a demand always to be “strong.” A child with this resource often learns early to endure more than their peers—to bear difficulties silently, avoid complaining, and assume responsibility beyond their age. A mother or father with this energy tends to teach through an example of resilience, calm, and reliability rather than harsh punishment or shouting.

The risk of this family energy arises when natural strength becomes a demand that a child show endurance beyond their age and capacity. Parents who value only stamina and self-control risk raising a child afraid to show weakness or ask for help. An adult raised under that demand may become excessively hard on themselves or, conversely, fear any expression of their own strength.

## Natural Strength

A child with eleventh energy usually demonstrates unusual endurance for their age. They persist with difficult tasks longer than their peers, rarely panic under stress, and naturally protect younger or more vulnerable people around them. This child needs their inner strength recognized, not constant demands to prove it again and again.

A mother or father with this energy naturally teaches through calm resilience. They do not panic in a crisis, show the child that difficulty can be endured without hysteria or aggression, and convey that strength is a support rather than a threat. This parenting style gives the child a rare gift: inner confidence in their own endurance.

Another family resource of this energy is the ability to tame conflict with gentle firmness. Both parents and children with this energy can calm a tense situation through steady presence without resorting to shouting or punishment, making the family atmosphere safer.

## Positive Expression

In the positive, a parent with eleventh energy creates a space where the child's strength is recognized and supported but never demanded beyond their capacity, while allowing them to show weakness, fatigue, or fear without shame or punishment.

A child with this energy in the positive receives recognition for their endurance without being required to prove it repeatedly, and knows they can be vulnerable at home without losing love or respect. They learn to trust their strength without fearing requests for help.

In the positive, a parent combines expectations with care. They ask for responsibility appropriate to the child's age, not beyond it, while showing by example that a strong person also has the right to rest and receive support.

Adult children with this energy in the positive can support their parents in old age without neglecting themselves. They offer practical and emotional help while preserving their own boundaries and resources rather than sacrificing themselves completely.

Another positive sign is teaching a child how to tame strong emotions: demonstrating that anger and irritation can be expressed calmly and safely instead of suppressed until an eruption or discharged onto others.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

As a mother, a woman in the positive gives her child a sense that it is safe to be strong while showing that vulnerability is not weakness, but part of a full life. As a daughter, she can support her parents through difficult periods while preserving her boundaries and not sacrificing her well-being to prove devotion constantly.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

As a father, a man in the positive passes on the value of endurance and reliability while allowing both himself and his children to show emotion without shame. As a son, he can support his parents through a difficult turn of fate with practical help, balancing care for them with care for himself.

## Negative Expression

In the negative, a child with this energy raised in a family that valued only endurance and self-control may develop a deep fear of showing weakness or become so accustomed to suppressing emotion that it later breaks through in unexpected outbursts of anger or anxiety.

A parent in the negative may demand that a child be “strong” beyond their age: not crying or complaining and handling difficulties alone that actually require adult support, leaving the child feeling that vulnerability is unacceptable.

In other cases, the negative appears as parental pressure through harsh, authoritarian methods: control, demands for unquestioning obedience, and punishment for weakness. This creates either chronic anxiety before authority or a tendency to reproduce the same pressure in adult life.

In relationships between adult children and parents, the negative appears as a silent standoff between two “strong” people, neither willing to acknowledge fatigue or ask for support first. The distance accumulates over years until someone dares to speak.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is a family environment where weakness or emotion was treated as an impermissible luxury. A second is a situation demanding extraordinary endurance from a child or adult without rest or support. A third is the childhood experience of a request for help going unheard, reinforcing the model that “I can rely only on myself.”

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step for parents is to recognize a child's strength without constantly demanding fresh proof. Praise not only endurance, but also honesty about difficulty. Consciously create a space where a child can say “This is hard for me” and receive support rather than disappointment.

For adult children with this energy, the second step is to practice honest conversation with parents about moments of childhood loneliness in difficulty rather than silently carrying that experience forward. The third step for both sides is to learn to ask for help first instead of waiting for the other person to admit weakness.

It is also useful to create family rituals for sharing difficulties without fear of judgment: regular, candid conversations about how things truly are instead of a formal “everything is fine.” Both children and parents can then feel that strength does not mean silent loneliness.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to learn to be strong and vulnerable at once: allowing yourself and your loved ones to take pride in endurance without sacrificing the right to support and openness. This is the path from silent, solitary endurance to mature strength that knows how to ask for help.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- As a parent, do I demand endurance from my child beyond their age and capacity?
- As a child, have I experienced being alone in difficulty when my request for help went unheard?
- To what extent do I allow myself and my loved ones to show weakness without shame?
- When did I last acknowledge my fatigue to someone close instead of enduring in silence?
- Is there a silent standoff between two “strong” people in my family, neither willing to speak first?
- What would strength unafraid of vulnerability mean to me?
- Do I teach my children to ask for help, or pass on my own fear of appearing weak?

## Practical Plan

1. This week, tell someone close to you, “This is hard for me right now,” instead of enduring silently.
2. If you are an adult who experienced childhood loneliness in difficulty, discuss it with a parent or write down the memories for yourself.
3. Praise a child or loved one not for endurance, but for honesty about the difficulty they are experiencing.
4. Notice one situation in which you or a loved one silently carries a burden, and be the first to offer specific support.
5. Create a regular time for honest family conversation about how things really are.
6. Notice when a loved one needs their vulnerability acknowledged rather than being told to “stay strong,” and give them that acknowledgment.
