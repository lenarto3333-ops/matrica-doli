# 3. The Empress — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In relationships with parents and children, this energy unfolds through care, physical warmth, and the ability to create the feeling of a safe, well-provided home. Relationships flow more easily when care is given generously but with respect for the other person's boundaries. They become a source of tension when care turns into overprotection or, conversely, when it is so lacking that a child or parent experiences emotional or material deprivation.

## Natural Strength

The natural resource of this energy in parent-child relationships is the ability to create a tangible, embodied sense of home: delicious food, a comfortable space, warm embraces, and a consistent, reliable presence. It is care that can be physically felt rather than merely heard in words.

A second strength is the patient cultivation of relationships, like tending a garden: understanding that trust and closeness cannot be forced but must be nurtured through years of consistent, sincere care and given time to ripen naturally.

A third strength is generosity in care that demands no immediate return—the capacity to give love and support while trusting that they will take root, even when the result is not immediately visible.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, a mother or father with this energy creates a home in which a child feels physically and emotionally safe: regularity, warmth, generous but not smothering care, and room for the child to grow at their own pace while feeling supported rather than pressured.

Positive care appears as the ability to offer support generously while respecting the boundaries of a child or parent: not imposing help where it was not requested, but offering it in a way that leaves the other person free to accept or decline.

The positive also brings the ability to teach a child to receive care and pleasure without guilt: allowing both oneself and the child to enjoy simple physical things—food, warmth, beauty—without turning them into rewards that must be earned.

Another sign of the positive state is an adult child's ability to appreciate parental care without experiencing it as a burden or an obligation to repay the same amount, but receiving it as a sincere gift that can be passed onward to one's own children or community.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

For a woman, maternal or filial care in the positive appears as a generous, warm presence that does not demand constant gratitude from a child or parent or require them to “justify” the care invested. She gives from love, not from a need to be needed.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

For a man, paternal or filial care in the positive appears as the ability to be a reliable, tangible source of support for the family—to provide comfort and stability while allowing both himself and the child to show warmth openly instead of hiding it behind practical care.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, care turns into overprotection. A mother or father attempts to control every aspect of the child's life in the name of caring, leaving no room for the personal experiences and mistakes necessary for growing up.

A second negative expression is care that demands repayment: parents give love and support as though these must be “paid for” through gratitude, attention, or conformity to expectations, while a child who fails to meet those expectations experiences emotional withdrawal.

A third expression is excessive material care in place of emotional closeness—substituting gifts, food, and financial provision for sincere connection when what the child or parent truly lacks is simple, warm, unconditional acceptance.

At a deeper negative level, the opposite extreme is also possible: emotional or material neglect so profound that the child grows up hungry for warmth and safety and attempts to compensate for it in adult life.

## What Can Pull This Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is the feeling that care remains unrecognized or devalued: a child or parent does not show the expected gratitude, and this is experienced as personal rejection of care that was sincerely invested.

A second trigger is a situation that requires relinquishing control over an adult child's life or receiving a parent's care without trying to assume responsibility for it. This is especially difficult for an energy accustomed to giving rather than receiving.

A third trigger is a prolonged lack of physical contact and warmth in the relationship due to distance or conflict, experienced acutely because this energy's care is so embodied in nature.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to honestly assess whether care in the relationship with a child or parent is given with respect for the other person's boundaries rather than as a way to control or fulfill one's own need to feel needed.

The second step is to consciously practice care that does not demand immediate gratitude or conformity to expectations, allowing it time to reveal its effects naturally.

The third step, if warmth and care were lacking in childhood, is to acknowledge that experience and consciously learn to give yourself the generous, embodied warmth that was missing instead of waiting for someone else to provide it.

The fourth step is to practice a balance between material care and emotional closeness, remembering that gifts and financial support cannot replace simple, sincere connection.

The fifth step is to learn to receive care from adult children or parents as generously as you are accustomed to giving it, allowing the relationship to become mutual rather than one-sided.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in relationships with parents and children is to learn to give care generously but without control, and to receive it without guilt or obligation. This means recognizing that true closeness is built not on the amount of care or material support invested, but on a warm, unconditional presence that gives the other person room to grow at their own pace.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- To what extent is my care for a child or parent given with respect for their boundaries, and to what extent is it a means of control?
- Do I expect gratitude or conformity in return for the care I invest?
- What did I lack in childhood—warmth and care, or, conversely, room for my own experience?
- Can I receive care from loved ones as generously as I am accustomed to giving it?
- Do I replace emotional closeness with material care—gifts and financial provision?
- What kind of home atmosphere do I want to create, or am I creating, for my child?

## Practical Plan

1. This week, show care for someone close without expecting gratitude or any particular response in return.
2. Write down one situation in which care turned into control, and consciously release part of that control.
3. Allow yourself to receive care from a child, parent, or partner without trying to immediately “repay” it in kind.
4. Spend time with a child or parent in a form of simple physical warmth—a shared meal, an embrace, or a walk—with no specific goal and no discussion of problems.
5. If care was lacking in childhood, offer yourself one generous, warm gesture this week as an act of self-care.
