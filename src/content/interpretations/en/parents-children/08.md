# 8. Justice — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In relationships between parents and children, this energy unfolds through honesty, equal treatment, and promises kept. A child with this energy is usually exceptionally sensitive to any injustice, real or perceived: favoritism in the family, a broken promise, or a punishment that seems disproportionate. As an adult, the person carries this same heightened sensitivity into parenting, where it can become either a great gift for honest and equitable upbringing or a source of exhausting scrutiny over the “fairness” of every family decision.

## Natural Strength

The natural resource of this energy in parent-child relationships is the ability to view a situation objectively, even when emotions are running high. A parent with this energy rarely delivers a verdict without hearing both sides, even when one of those sides is their own child. Children trust such parents as honest arbiters in disputes with one another.

A second strength is consistency. A child in such a family usually knows exactly what to expect from a particular action because the rules are applied equally to everyone rather than according to the adult's mood. This predictability gives children a profound sense of security.

A third strength is the ability to admit being wrong to a child. A parent with this energy can honestly say, “I treated you unfairly,” and put matters right instead of defending their authority at any cost. This teaches the child that responsibility is not a punishment but a natural part of honest relationships.

## Positive Expression

In the positive, a parent with this energy treats children with equal honesty and consistency, explaining rules and consequences clearly rather than imposing them as adult whims. The child feels that they will be heard, even if the final decision does not go their way.

Care in the positive is expressed as a willingness to admit mistakes to a child and correct an injustice as soon as it becomes apparent, instead of defending one's position on principle. Parents are more likely to ask, “Did I treat you fairly?” than insist on their own infallibility.

The positive expression also brings a healthy attitude toward rules. The family has clear, consistently applied agreements that the child experiences not as punishment but as support, because they are the same for everyone and do not change with someone's mood.

Another sign of a positive state is the ability of adult children and parents to speak honestly about past hurts and fairly acknowledge each side's share of responsibility, instead of leaving one party permanently “guilty” and the other permanently “wronged.”

### Expression in Women in the Positive

In women, maternal or filial care in the positive appears as honest, equal treatment of every child in the family, without hidden favorites, and as the ability to admit fault to parents or children without excessive shame. She builds trust through consistency between her words and actions.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

In men, paternal or filial care in the positive appears as principle tempered with warmth. He honors agreements with his children as rigorously as he expects them to, and admits when he is wrong without losing authority. His word carries weight precisely because it is always matched by his actions.

## Negative Expression

In the negative, a keen sense of justice turns into constant comparison among children—who received more attention, who was punished less, who is the “favorite.” Even if parents consciously try to avoid this, children still sense the hidden weighing and measuring.

A second negative expression is the rigid, almost judicial application of rules without regard for context or the child's feelings. Punishment “in the name of fairness” becomes more important than understanding why the child acted that way.

A third expression is the accumulation of childhood grievances against parents over experienced injustice. The adult carries these grievances for years, mentally passing judgment on their parents for every instance of unequal treatment instead of either discussing it directly or letting it go.

At a deeper negative level, the opposite scenario is also possible: an adult child or parent is so afraid of repeating family injustice that they fall into excessive, exhausting concern with making every detail equal, turning upbringing or family relationships into an accounting exercise in attention and resources.

## What Can Pull the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is the sense that the child—or the person themselves as a child—was treated unfairly: a broken promise, punishment without explanation, or a clear difference in how children in the family were treated. A second trigger is the absence of clear, consistent family rules, where something permitted today was punished yesterday. A third trigger is a moment when an adult child or parent feels that their position is not acknowledged despite evidence that seems obvious to them.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to assess honestly whether children in the family are actually treated unequally, or whether this is an old childhood feeling that should be examined through direct conversation instead of carried in silence for years. The second step is to learn to apply rules consistently while accounting for context: justice is not the same punishment under every circumstance, but an honest consideration of each child's situation.

The third step is to practice admitting when one is wrong to children or parents as soon as it becomes apparent, instead of defending a position on principle. The fourth step is to bring conscious closure to old childhood grievances through conversation or inner forgiveness that does not require parents to admit fault before the burden can be released. The fifth step is to distinguish genuine concern for equality from compulsive tallying of attention and resources among children or generations.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in relationships with parents and children is to learn to be an honest and fair witness to family life rather than its constant judge. This means recognizing that true fairness in a family is not expressed through a perfectly equal distribution of attention or punishment, but through honesty, consistency, and a willingness to admit one's mistakes to those one loves.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I carry a childhood grievance against my parents over an injustice I still keep silent about?
- How consistently do I apply rules with children or in my relationship with my parents—equally for everyone rather than according to my mood?
- Can I admit that I was wrong to a child or my parents without feeling that I lose authority?
- Do I compare children with one another, or myself with my siblings, through the lens of “who received more”?
- Has my concern for fairness become compulsive tallying of attention and resources?
- What would genuinely forgiving an old family injustice mean to me?

## Practical Plan

1. Write down one unfair situation from childhood that you still carry and decide whether to discuss it directly or consciously let it go.
2. Check one family rule for consistency: is it applied equally to all family members, regardless of circumstances or mood?
3. Admit to a child or your parents one mistake or unfair decision that has never been addressed.
4. Notice one situation this week in which you compare children or relatives, and consciously redirect your attention to each person's individual needs.
5. Have a conversation with a child or your parents about what fair treatment in the family means to them instead of making assumptions.
6. Practice one act of forgiveness for an old family hurt that does not require the other party to admit fault.
