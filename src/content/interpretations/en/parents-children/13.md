# 13. Death — Relationships with Parents and Children

## Short summary

In family relationships, this energy values the right to change and completion—a space in which a child or adult family member can close a phase that has exhausted itself and begin a new one without holding on to it out of guilt or fear. A father or mother with this energy usually helps a child go through important changes - a move, a divorce in the family, a change of school or social circle - and a child with this resource often lets go of the old and adapts to the new more easily than others. Risk is when this ability to complete turns into abrupt, ill-advised family breaks or, conversely, panicked clinging to outdated relationship patterns out of fear of change.

## Natural force

A child with this energy usually has a natural ability to quickly adapt to changes in the family: a move, the appearance of a new family member, a change in the usual way of life do not cause such strong resistance in him as in other children, because he intuitively feels that after the completion of one stage, a new, often more interesting one comes.

A father or mother with this energy naturally teaches a child that ending is not a disaster: when something changes or ends in the family, they show by their own example how to say goodbye to the old with dignity and meet the new with trust, rather than freezing the family in fear of change.

Another resource of this energy in the family is the ability to recognize in time when some family model, habit or way of interaction has exhausted itself and needs updating: this person, child or adult, is the first to notice that the family script is outdated and dares to propose a change.

## Manifestation in the plus

On the plus side, the father or mother helps the child go through important life endings - saying goodbye to a favorite toy, school, friends when moving, changing the usual way of life - not hiding the fact of change from her, but explaining it clearly and supporting her in the process of saying goodbye. This fosters in the child a healthy attitude towards change as a natural part of life, and not as a threat.

Positive family communication includes a willingness to recognize when some family custom, rule, or way of interacting has worn itself out and needs updating: parents don't cling to outdated parenting patterns just because their parents did, but consciously revise what no longer works for their own child.

In the role of an adult child in relation to his own parents, this energy is able to complete the outdated model of relations with parents - co-dependence, excessive accountability, a child's role in conversations - moving to a more mature, equal format of communication, not breaking the connection, but transforming it.

Another sign of a positive state is the ability of the family to go through important transformations together, supporting each other: closing the family stage, changing the composition of the family, the child's transition to a new age, perceiving this not as a threat to stability, but as a natural development that brings people together, not apart.

### Manifestation in women is positive

As a mother, a woman with this positive energy helps her child go through difficult goodbyes and changes, not belittling her feelings or rushing the grieving process, but giving her time and support to face the new stage with confidence. She shows by her own example that completion does not mean the end of love or stability.

She is able to timely complete outdated models of upbringing or interaction that suited the child at a younger age, but are already tight for a teenager or an adult child, consciously renewing relationships instead of holding on to a familiar, but already uncomfortable format.

### Manifestation in the husband is positive

In the role of a father, a man with this positive energy is not afraid to admit to his children when some family arrangement or rule is no longer working, and decisively initiates change, showing by his own example that renewal is a sign of family strength, not weakness. It helps children see completion as part of natural development.

He teaches children to go through difficult goodbyes with dignity - moving, changing schools, breaking up friendships - supporting them in the process, not rushing to "quickly forget and move on", and at the same time skillfully leads the family to a new stage when the time comes.

## Manifestation in the minus

In the negative role of parents, the ability to complete turns into either sharp, traumatic breaks - a sudden move without explanation to the child, a sudden change in the family structure without preparation - or to the opposite extreme: panicked clinging to outdated models of upbringing and relationships, even when the child has clearly grown out of them.

Another manifestation of the negative is the parental inability to allow the child to complete its own stage of development: parents continue to treat the adult child as a small one, without recognizing that the role of the guardian has already exhausted itself and needs to be transformed into a more equal relationship.

In the role of an adult child, the minus manifests itself as being stuck in an outdated model of relations with parents - constant accountability, a child's role, excessive dependence on parental approval - despite the fact that a person has long grown up and needs a different format of communication. The break here either does not occur at all, or occurs abruptly and painfully, without an attempt to transform the relationship gradually.

In a deeper negative, a family pattern of total denial of change is also possible: the family pretends that nothing has changed, even when it is obvious that the child has grown up, the parents have aged, or the circumstances are radically different, and this denial accumulates tension, which eventually breaks through in a sharp, destructive conflict.

## What can be negative

The strongest trigger for parents is a situation that requires recognition: the method of upbringing or the model of relationships that worked before are no longer suitable for this child at a new stage of his development, which is perceived as a threat to his own parental competence. The second trigger is a family change that comes suddenly and without preparation, causing the family to react in panic instead of a conscious transition. The third trigger for an adult child is the moment when it is necessary to end the old pattern of dependence on parents, which activates a deep fear of disconnection altogether.

## How to return energy to the plus side

The first step for parents is to learn to distinguish between a change that a child really needs at a new stage of development and a betrayal of the usual way of life for the sake of change: it is worth regularly checking whether the current parenting model corresponds to the real age and needs of the child. The second step is to prepare important family endings in advance, explaining to the child what is happening and giving him time to experience the change, rather than confronting the fact.

The third step for an adult child is to realize which model of relationship with parents has already exhausted itself, and start gradually, and not abruptly, to build a new, adult format of communication. The fourth step is to practice open conversation in the family about what has already changed and needs to be acknowledged, instead of silently denying the obvious transformations.

It is also helpful to have a regular family moment of conscious stocktaking—what has ended, what has begun—recognizing change openly, provided that this ending happens calmly and gradually, rather than in a panic or sudden break.

## The main task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to learn to go through the necessary family transformations consciously and carefully, without freezing the relationship in an outdated model and without destroying the connection with a sharp break. This applies to both roles at the same time: to be the parent who helps the child complete milestones and meet new ones with dignity, and to be the adult child who learns to transform the relationship with his parents, and not either to be stuck in them forever or to break them abruptly and painfully.

## Questions for self-reflection

- What model of relationships or upbringing in my family has already exhausted itself, but we still cling to it out of habit?
- Do I help the child (or have they helped me) say goodbye to important milestones in a dignified way, or do we avoid this topic?
- To what extent does my model of relationship with my parents correspond to who I am now and not to who I was as a child?

## Practical plan

1. Write down one model of family relations or upbringing that has already exhausted itself, and describe what an updated version of it could be.
2. Have a conversation with the child or relative about the important change that is coming, explaining it in advance, rather than putting it before the fact.
3. If you are an adult child stuck in an outdated pattern of relationship with parents, write down one specific step towards a more mature, equal format of communication.
4. Notice one time this week when the family avoids acknowledging an obvious change, and gently call it out loud.
5. Arrange a family conversation "what has ended, what is beginning", in which everyone can name a change that is important to them.
6. Write a short letter (you don't have to send it) to the parents or the child, in which you honestly describe which part of the relationship you want to end and which part you want to start over.
