# 12. The Hanged Man — Relationships with Parents and Children

## Brief Essence

In family bonds, this energy values the right to pause—a space where a child or adult family member can stop, think, and view the situation differently before acting or responding. A parent with this energy rarely demands an immediate reaction, while a child with this resource often decides more slowly but more accurately once ready. The risk arises when this healthy need for a pause turns into continual postponement of important family conversations or decisions, leaving the family in years of uncertainty that no one dares to end.

## Natural Strength

A child with this energy usually has a natural ability to view family situations from an angle unavailable to other relatives. They notice what adults overlook through habit or haste and often offer unexpectedly wise observations, although they may not be ready to voice them immediately.

A parent with this energy naturally gives a child time to move at their own pace. They do not rush a decision or demand an instant reaction to a difficult event, but allow the child to process and consider it in their own rhythm. This gives the child a sense that their inner process is respected rather than forced into a timetable convenient for adults.

Another family resource is the ability to endure uncertainty without panic. When a situation is difficult and no answer is yet available, this person—child or adult—can remain a calm support for others until matters become clearer instead of adding more anxiety to the family.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, a parent gives the child space to pause before important decisions. Rather than forcing an instant choice, they create conditions in which the child can think, seek advice, and view the situation from different sides. This develops trust in the child’s own thinking pace instead of an anxious habit of deciding on the run merely to keep up with adult expectations.

Positive family communication respects different views. Parents encourage the child to share how they see a situation even when it differs from the adults’ obvious, familiar perspective, and this openness to another viewpoint often saves the family from mistaken decisions made in haste.

As an adult child in relation to one’s parents, the positive expression can give the family time to change without demanding immediate acknowledgment or transformation, while avoiding becoming trapped in endless waiting for that change. The adult child builds a life of their own while leaving parents space to move at their own pace.

Another positive sign is the family’s ability to pause together before an important decision instead of acting from inertia or pressure: a family council, shared reflection, and willingness to hear everyone’s position, including the child’s, before proceeding.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

As a mother, a woman with this energy creates a home where a child is not ashamed to say, “I need to think,” and where the pause is treated as a normal part of growing up rather than evasion of responsibility. She teaches the child to trust their own pace without comparison with children who decide more quickly.

She can be a quiet support for the family in uncertain periods, staying calm when other relatives become anxious, while not allowing herself to remain forever in the role of waiting when the time to act arrives.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

As a father, a man with this energy does not pressure a child to be decisive and quick in everything. By example, he shows that a thoughtful, slower decision is often more valuable than a rushed one. He openly acknowledges to his children when he needs time to think, dispelling the myth that an adult must always have an immediate answer.

He teaches children to view situations from several sides by asking questions instead of supplying ready-made conclusions. At the same time, he knows how to end reflection with a concrete decision the family can understand, without leaving loved ones in uncertainty longer than necessary.

## Expression in the Negative

In a negative parental role, the healthy need for a pause becomes chronic postponement of important decisions about the child. Questions involving education, health, school conflicts, or family changes are “considered” for years but never receive a concrete response or action, and the child lives in uncertainty they cannot change themselves.

Another negative expression is parental passivity when the child needs clear intervention. Instead of taking a position in a conflict, establishing a boundary, or making a difficult decision, the parent “waits for everything to resolve itself,” leaving the child without protection or guidance precisely when it is most needed.

As an adult child, the negative appears as being trapped in waiting. A person waits for years for parents to change, acknowledge mistakes, or become different, instead of building a life regardless of that expectation. The wait can last for decades, consuming energy that could have supported personal development.

At a deeper negative level, a pattern of quiet family self-sacrifice may arise. One family member—a parent or adult child—postpones their own life for others for years, telling themselves this is love, although it exhausts everyone involved, including those for whom the sacrifice is supposedly made.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger for parents is a situation requiring a rapid, clear decision about a child when this energy’s natural pace cannot match the urgency, provoking either panic or paralysis through analysis. Another trigger is a family conflict that requires taking a definite position rather than remaining a neutral observer, which can feel like a threat to family peace. For an adult child, a third trigger is contact with parents that activates a longstanding, deeply rooted wait for acknowledgment or change that never came.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

For parents, the first step is learning to distinguish a pause that gives the child room to think from a delay that leaves them without necessary protection or an answer. Check regularly whether “we are still thinking” has become a way to avoid a difficult decision. The second step is setting deadlines for important family decisions, even when this requires action without complete certainty.

For an adult child, the third step is recognizing how much of one’s own life has been surrendered to waiting for parents to change, then beginning to make plans and decisions regardless of whether that change ever occurs. The fourth is practicing open family conversations about exactly what is missing before a delayed decision can be made, instead of waiting silently for matters to clarify on their own.

It is also useful to create regular moments when the family consciously pauses before important decisions—a short conversation in which everyone can express their view—provided the pause always ends with a concrete step rather than dissolving into another period of waiting.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to combine respect for one’s own and others’ pace with readiness to act when loved ones need not another pause, but a clear position or decision. This applies to both roles at once: being a parent whose ability to stop and see differently does not deprive the child of support, and being an adult child who learns to live their own life without waiting forever for a change they cannot control.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I give my child room to pause before deciding, or demand an immediate reaction?
- How many important family decisions have been “under consideration” for years without a concrete result?
- Am I waiting for a change from my parents that has already taken a very long time?
- How willing am I to take a clear position in a family conflict instead of remaining a neutral observer?
- Am I sacrificing my own life for other family members without a genuine need to do so?
- What would change in my family if we brought postponed conversations to concrete decisions more often?

## Practical Plan

1. Write down one family decision that has been “under consideration” for more than a month and set a specific deadline for making it.
2. Have a short conversation with your child or relative in which you consciously give them time to express their view of a difficult situation before offering your solution.
3. If you are an adult child waiting for your parents to change, write down one concrete step in your own life that you will take regardless of whether that change occurs.
4. Notice one moment this week when a family pause becomes procrastination, and consciously take a step toward a decision.
5. Hold a brief family conversation before an important decision in which everyone has a voice, and end it with a concrete conclusion.
6. Write a short letter—not necessarily to send—to a parent or child, honestly describing what you expect and how much longer you are willing to wait.
