# 7. The Chariot — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In relationships with parents and children, this energy unfolds through themes of direction, independence, and the right to move at one's own pace. Relationships flow more easily when parents give a child room to follow their own path, offering support without directing every step. They become a source of tension when care turns into control or, conversely, when the child feels compelled to compete constantly for attention or recognition.

## Natural Strength

The natural resource of this energy in parent-child relationships is the ability to give a child a sense of direction and support their initiative without taking over the reins. These are parents who know how to be a steady presence along the journey rather than a brake or a tow rope.

Another strength is encouraging independence: understanding that a child learns to steer their own life precisely when they are given genuine responsibility and the right to make their own decisions, even when those decisions are not always perfect.

A third strength is the ability to mobilize for the family at a critical moment: determination that emerges when a child or parent truly needs protection or support, rather than being wasted on petty, everyday competition.

## Positive Expression

In its positive expression, a parent with this energy supports a child's independence, allowing them to choose their own pace and direction while remaining a dependable source of support in difficult moments. The child feels that their initiative is welcomed rather than suppressed through comparison or excessive control.

Care in the positive manifests as the ability to trust a child with the right to make their own decisions and mistakes, without turning every step into something to monitor or judge. Parents are more likely to ask, "What do you need to move forward?" than to dictate what that movement should look like.

The positive expression also brings healthy competition: parents and children can set goals together and support one another's achievements without turning the relationship into a constant comparison of "who is better" or "who has achieved more."

Another sign of a positive state is an adult child's ability to build their own independent path while preserving a warm connection with their parents, rather than treating independence as a break in the relationship or a betrayal of family expectations.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

In women, maternal or daughterly care in the positive manifests as sincere support for a child or parent's independence, without the need to prove her own competence constantly by controlling their life. She gives them room to move while remaining a dependable source of support.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

In men, paternal or filial care in the positive manifests as the ability to model determination while respecting the pace of a child or parent, without imposing his own route as the only correct one. He supports their initiative instead of taking over the reins.

## Negative Expression

In its negative expression, care turns into excessive control: a parent tries to direct the child's every step under the guise of protection or experience, leaving no room for the child's own decisions and the mistakes essential to growing up.

Another negative expression is turning the relationship into a constant competition: parents compare the child with other children or with their own achievements, while the child feels compelled to prove their worth continually through results rather than simply being accepted.

A third expression is imposing one's own unrealized path on the child: parents may push the child toward goals that actually reflect their own ambitions or unfinished aspirations rather than the child's genuine wishes.

At a deeper negative level, the opposite extreme is also possible—a conflict over autonomy in which a child or adult child fights so fiercely for independence that they sever contact with their parents instead of separating in a healthy way while preserving the connection.

## What Can Pull the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is the feeling that a child or adult child is choosing a path the parents consider "wrong" or dangerous, creating a powerful impulse to intervene and take control.

Another trigger is any situation that requires acknowledging an adult child's independence and letting go of the familiar role of leader or protector. This is especially difficult for an energy accustomed to leading and controlling the process.

A third trigger is comparing the child—or one's own parenting achievements—with other families, which intensifies a hidden sense of competition where simple support should be.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to assess honestly how much care for a child or parent comes with trust in their ability to make decisions independently, and how much serves as a way to control the outcome.

The second step is to distinguish consciously between one's own ambitions and unrealized dreams and the child's genuine wishes, supporting the child's direction rather than projecting one's own onto them.

The third step is to practice support without comparison: recognize a child or parent's progress in relation to who they were before, not in relation to other people or families.

The fourth step is to allow oneself and other family members the right to make mistakes along the way, understanding that an independent, imperfect step is more valuable than a perfect step taken under someone else's control.

The fifth step is to learn to remain a source of support for an adult child or parent even when their path differs from what was expected, preserving the connection instead of trying to regain control.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in relationships with parents and children is to learn to support another person's movement without trying to direct its course. This means recognizing that genuine care is expressed not through control over every step, but through trust in a child or parent's ability to find the path that is right for them on their own.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- How much does my care for my child or parents come from trust in their independence, and how much is it a way to control them?
- Do I compare my child or my own achievements with others instead of supporting individual progress?
- Are the goals I impose on my child or parents actually my own unrealized ambitions?
- Do I allow myself and others the right to make mistakes while moving independently?
- Do I remain a source of support for loved ones even when their path differs from what I expected?
- What atmosphere of support, rather than control, would I like to create in my family?

## Practical Plan

1. This week, consciously refrain from offering advice or intervening in a decision that your child or parents are capable of making themselves.
2. Write down one situation in which care turned into control, and consciously release part of that control.
3. Identify one unrealized ambition of your own that you may be unconsciously projecting onto your child, and separate it from the child's genuine wishes.
4. Support your child or parents in one achievement without comparing them with other people or families.
5. Have a conversation with your adult child or parents about the support they genuinely need right now, rather than the support you habitually offer.
