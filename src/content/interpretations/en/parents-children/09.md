# 9. The Hermit — Relationships with Parents and Children

## In Brief

In parent-child relationships, the ninth energy is expressed through a need for depth, quiet, and personal space that can easily be mistaken for detachment. A child with this resource often learns early to be alone with themselves—to read, think, and observe—and finds inner support precisely in this solitude. A parent with this energy tends to teach by modeling a deep, thoughtful approach to life rather than through constant emotional display.

The risk of this energy within a family arises when the need for depth and solitude becomes a barrier to genuine closeness. A child whose seriousness was never truly heard may grow into an adult afraid of being fully seen. A parent who hides behind intellectual distance risks raising a child who feels emotionally alone beside an adult who is physically present.

## Natural Strength

A child with the ninth energy usually develops a rich inner world early. They can spend hours playing or pursuing an activity alone, ask questions beyond their years, and notice details and subtext that adults often miss. This child needs less external stimulation than others and more room for their own reflection.

A parent with this energy naturally teaches through an example of independent thinking and respect for the child’s inner world. They do not pressure the child to be constantly visible or surrounded by company, allowing them to explore the world at their own pace. This parenting style gives the child a rare gift: trust in their own ability to think independently without relying on constant external approval.

Another resource this energy brings to the family is the capacity to be the adult or child who speaks less than others, but whose words carry weight for that very reason. The family learns to listen to this person on important rather than trivial matters because they know that this person does not speak merely to fill the silence.

## Positive Expression

In the positive, a parent with the ninth energy creates a space where the child’s seriousness, curiosity, and need for quiet are respected rather than treated as problems to be corrected. Through their own example, they teach the child to value deep relationships over a broad circle of superficial acquaintances, showing that quality matters more than quantity.

A child with this energy in the positive receives permission from their parents to be themselves—thoughtful and not always ready to join a noisy group immediately—while also feeling that this does not make them less loving or less connected to the family. They learn to ask for time alone without guilt, while also returning from solitude to loved ones rather than using it as a permanent escape.

In the positive, a parent knows how to be present deeply rather than frequently. A brief but truly attentive conversation with the child means more to both of them than hours of formal shared activity without genuine connection. They notice when the child needs advice or support and offer it thoughtfully rather than reactively.

Adult children with this energy in the positive become the people their parents approach for balanced advice on difficult matters—not because they are the loudest in the family, but because their perspective is thoughtful and honest. They know how to support their parents in old age with tactful, unobtrusive care while also respecting their own need for personal space.

Another sign of the positive is the ability to teach a child how to be alone without fear: to show that solitude can be a resource for creativity, reflection, and renewal rather than punishment or isolation.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

As a mother, a woman in the positive gives her child space for their own, often unconventional pace of development without forcing them to be as sociable or loud as other children. As a daughter, she can maintain a deep and honest bond with her parents while preserving her own boundaries and need for personal space without guilt.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

As a father, a man in the positive passes on to his children the value of independent thought and a deep approach to their work, while making time for genuine rather than merely formal presence in their lives. As a son, he can support his parents through difficult decisions, maintaining a calm, balanced position even when other family members respond emotionally.

## Negative Expression

In the negative, a child with this energy whose seriousness was not heard or whose “strangeness” was mocked closes themselves off much earlier and more completely than necessary. They stop sharing their inner world with their parents because experience has already taught them that it will not be valued. Over the years, this distance becomes habitual, making it difficult for the grown child to open up even to those they love.

A parent in the negative may be physically nearby but emotionally unavailable. Immersed in their own thoughts, analysis, work, or inner world, they miss moments when the child needs simple, warm presence rather than wise advice. The child learns not to trouble the parent with their feelings because they sense that the adult is “busy with something more important.”

In other cases, the negative appears as parental pressure toward forced sociability. Worried about the child being “withdrawn,” adults push them into social groups, clubs, and noisy interaction without making room for their natural need for quiet. This creates either rebellious and even more rigid withdrawal or a habit of pretending to adapt while hiding true feelings still more deeply.

In relationships between adult children and parents, the negative appears as a silent distance maintained for years. Both sides may love each other, but neither takes the first step toward an open conversation because each fears being misunderstood or rejected for showing vulnerability.

## What Can Pull the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is an experience in which a child’s seriousness, depth, or need for solitude was mocked, called strange, or treated as a problem to be “fixed” through greater sociability. The second trigger is a family environment with no right to boundaries or personal space, where privacy is constantly violated. The third is the emotional coldness or physical unavailability of a parent, which forces the child to learn early to rely only on themselves and reinforces the pattern: “I will handle it alone because relying on others is risky.”

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step for parents is to learn to distinguish a child’s healthy need for solitude from anxious isolation. The former gives the child energy and calm; the latter drains them and is accompanied by sadness or fear. Take an interest in the child’s inner world without pressuring them to reveal everything immediately, allowing them to feel that the door is open whenever they are ready.

The second step for adult children with this energy is to practice small, measured expressions of openness with their parents: one sincere conversation, one shared memory or experience, instead of either complete silence or immediate, exhaustive disclosure. The third step, for both sides, is to agree on a form of closeness that fits their actual needs rather than conventional expectations. Brief but deep conversations are often more valuable than frequent, superficial contact.

It is also helpful to create shared rituals of quiet presence consciously—reading beside one another or taking a walk without any obligation to talk—which foster connection without demanding constant verbal contact. This is especially valuable in families where both the parent and child carry this energy: closeness here is built not through the number of words, but through the quality of the silence shared together.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to learn to be deeply present without hiding behind distance: to allow yourself and your loved ones genuine vulnerability in the few moments when it is needed, instead of continually protecting your inner world with silence. This is the path from the family’s solitary observer to its quiet, dependable center.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- As a parent, do I give my child room for their natural need for solitude without pressuring them to be more sociable?
- As a child, have I experienced having my seriousness or need for quiet mocked or misunderstood?
- How emotionally available am I to my loved ones, and do I hide behind busyness or inner analysis?
- When did I last share something truly personal with my parents or children rather than something merely practical?
- Is there a silent distance in my family relationships that neither side dares to break?
- What would genuine closeness that does not require constant words mean to me?
- Do I teach my children that being alone can be a resource rather than a punishment?

## Practical Plan

1. Notice one situation in which you, as a parent, pressure your child to be more sociable than feels natural to them, and consciously release that pressure.
2. If you are an adult with this energy, share something personal—a thought, memory, or experience—with one of your parents this week.
3. Invite a loved one to spend time together in quiet presence without any obligation to talk, such as taking a walk or reading beside each other.
4. Notice a moment when a loved one wants to share something important and give them your full, unhurried attention.
5. Write down one family subject that is never discussed openly and consider whether you are ready to take the first step toward talking about it.
6. Create a small, regular ritual of connection with your child or parents that does not depend on mood or busyness.
