# 16. The Tower — Relationships with Parents and Children

## Brief Essence

In family relationships, this energy may manifest through sudden, unsettling moments of truth. Something that has long been concealed or left unspoken can surface unexpectedly, forcing everyone to reconsider the familiar pattern of the relationship. A parent with this energy usually has the inner strength to survive and manage serious family crises, yet may act abruptly and without warning once their patience runs out. A child with this resource may encounter instability or major family changes early in life and have to learn how to cope when familiar structures fall apart. The risk appears when this strength turns into chronic conflict and angry outbursts, or into traumatic tension that children carry into adulthood.

## Natural Strength

A child with this energy often develops an unusually mature resilience. Even after a serious family upheaval—parental separation, relocation, or a financial crisis—they may find the ability to adapt and keep moving forward. When adults acknowledge and support the child's experience instead of dismissing it, this early resilience can become a lasting inner resource.

A parent with this energy is naturally capable of decisive action during a family crisis. When the situation calls for a difficult and unpopular change—ending a harmful connection, moving to a safer place, or acknowledging a painful truth—they can take that step even when other family members would rather preserve the appearance of stability.

Another resource of this energy is the willingness to tell the truth when others would prefer to remain silent. A parent with this energy rarely allows family problems to simmer unspoken for years. Although their honesty may sometimes sound harsh, it can protect the family from deeper and more prolonged damage.

## Expression in the Positive

In its positive expression, a parent uses their ability to act decisively in a crisis to protect the family from prolonged hidden harm. They are willing to name a painful truth or take a difficult but necessary step, guided by care for the child rather than irritation or a desire to punish.

Healthy family communication includes facing crises honestly and together. When a loss, conflict, or major change occurs, parents do not hide it behind a facade of artificial calm. Instead, they explain what is happening in an age-appropriate way. This shows the child that even difficult events can be endured when family members support one another.

For an adult child in relation to their parents, the positive side of this energy helps them acknowledge past family crises without minimizing or dramatizing them. They can draw resilience from those experiences without allowing trauma to determine every later decision. They can also build relationships in which honesty about difficult subjects does not create constant tension.

Another sign of a positive state is the family's ability to turn a crisis they have survived into shared strength. After a difficult period, family members do not retreat into silent distance or mutual blame. They look for a way to become closer and more honest with one another than they were before the ordeal.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

As a mother, a woman expressing this energy positively can become a source of stability during the hardest moments of family life. She does not surrender to panic and remains calm enough for the children to feel that, even when everything around them seems to be collapsing, a responsible adult is present and able to make decisions about their safety.

She can speak honestly with children about difficult family events—separation, loss, or financial hardship—without hiding the truth behind false reassurance or burdening them with adult responsibilities. Her strength lies in finding the right balance between honesty and protection.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

As a father, a man expressing this energy positively can take responsibility during a family crisis without directing his panic or anger at his children or partner. He acts decisively when concrete action is required while remaining emotionally available, so the children are not afraid to come to him with their concerns.

Through his example, he teaches children that a crisis is not necessarily the end but a difficult period that can be navigated with dignity and may ultimately make a person stronger. This lesson is conveyed less through words than through the way he handles family trials himself.

## Expression in the Negative

In its negative expression, a parent's decisiveness can turn into chronic conflict and angry outbursts. Adults may lash out at children when under stress or engage in harsh, destructive arguments in front of them. The child then grows up under the constant threat of an unpredictable explosion, even if the family appears stable from the outside.

Another negative pattern is concealing a family crisis until the last possible moment, when the truth erupts suddenly and causes even more pain. Parents may remain silent for years about serious financial or relationship problems, only for the children to learn about a separation, debt, or another upheaval without warning. The sudden revelation can itself become a traumatic experience.

For an adult child, the negative side may appear as chronic hypervigilance and an expectation of disaster. Someone who experienced sudden family upheavals in childhood may struggle to trust stability even when there is no actual threat. Constantly preparing for another blow can exhaust the person and may also affect their own children.

At a deeper level, the family may repeat the same crisis pattern. Similar conflicts, ruptures, or financial collapses recur because the previous upheaval was never understood or processed. A child who observes this cycle may come to see catastrophe as a normal part of family life.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger for parents is accumulated, unspoken stress that eventually spills onto the children as anger or conflict instead of being addressed separately. A second trigger is the family habit of hiding problems until the last moment: when the truth finally emerges, it feels like a catastrophe rather than a difficulty that can be discussed and managed. For an adult child, situations resembling childhood upheavals may activate disproportionate anxiety or defensive aggression even in relatively safe circumstances.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

The first step for parents is to monitor their level of internal tension and find safe outlets away from the children, such as talking with a partner, seeking psychological support, or engaging in physical activity. This reduces the risk of releasing stress through angry outbursts in family relationships. The second step is to practice gradual, age-appropriate honesty about family difficulties instead of allowing silence to build until the truth erupts.

For an adult child, the third step is to learn to distinguish a real threat from an anxious echo of childhood upheaval. Where possible, it may be helpful to work consciously through traumatic experiences rather than silently carrying them forward. The fourth step is to discuss past crises openly as a family, acknowledging the pain they caused while also recognizing that the family managed to survive them together.

It is also useful to notice whether the family atmosphere is governed by chronic fear of the next blow. Deliberately creating moments of calm and predictability shows children that not every day has to become another test of endurance.

## Main Task

The main task of this energy in family relationships is to move through unavoidable crises honestly and with dignity, without concealing them until a sudden explosion and without discharging accumulated tension onto the people closest to you. Genuine family closeness is not built on the illusion that crises will never occur, but on the confidence that the family can face them together and become more honest in the process.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Do I direct accumulated tension at my children or parents through sudden outbursts?
- How honestly does my family discuss difficulties, or do we wait for the truth to erupt on its own?
- Which past family crisis still affects my ability to trust stability today?
- What happens inside me before an emotional outburst, and which early signs of tension can I notice?
- Do children receive clear, age-appropriate explanations of major family changes?
- Which rules help our family maintain emotional and physical safety during conflict?
- What resilience have we gained from the difficulties we have already survived?

## Practical Plan

1. Write down one situation in which your response to family stress was harsher than the circumstances required, and consider the real source of that tension.
2. Identify a family problem that is currently being avoided and plan how to begin discussing it gradually and safely.
3. Talk with your child or parents about a crisis the family has survived, acknowledging the pain while also recognizing the resilience you developed together.
4. If you are an adult child, identify one situation that triggers disproportionate anxiety because of childhood experience and take one step toward consciously processing it.
5. If you are a parent, choose a separate and safe way to manage your stress—conversation, support, or a regular practice—so that you do not transfer it to your children.
6. Hold a family conversation about how you navigated past difficulties and what helped you become closer afterward.
