# 9. The Hermit — Common Relationship Difficulties

## In Brief

In relationships, the ninth energy most often encounters the same problem: a partner struggles to reach through the distance that the person builds almost automatically. This does not come from indifference, but from a deeply rooted habit of processing feelings alone before sharing them—or instead of sharing them at all.

## Natural Strength

In a resourceful state, this energy brings a rare quality to relationships: thoughtfulness. A partner receives a considered response rather than a reactive one, support grounded in a genuine understanding of the situation, and space that is neither demanding nor suffocating. The ability to analyze a conflict instead of drowning in it emotionally can be enormously valuable to a couple.

## Positive Expression

In the positive, you know how to combine a need for personal space with genuine presence in the relationship. You can say honestly, “I need time to think; I’ll come back to this conversation in an hour”—and you truly do return. You share the conclusions of your inner reflection with your partner instead of keeping them to yourself as private property.

## Negative Expression

In the negative, you retreat during conflict instead of remaining in contact: silence, a curt “everything is fine,” or physical or emotional disappearance at the very moment your partner most needs a conversation. Feelings are intellectualized—instead of saying “I’m hurt,” you analyze the situation, maintaining emotional distance even while discussing emotions. Over time, your partner begins to feel unnecessary in their own relationship.

## What Can Pull the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is the sense that your emotions will be used against you if you reveal them, as well as pressure from a partner to respond or decide immediately without time to think. Past experiences of having deep feelings mocked or dismissed reinforce the habit of hiding them behind analysis.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to agree with your partner on an honest signal for taking a pause instead of disappearing into silence: a specific phrase and a specific time when you will return to the conversation. The second is to practice naming the feeling in one word before analyzing it: first, “I’m sad” or “I’m angry,” and only then an explanation of why.

It is also helpful to share what is happening inside you regularly, even when there is no conflict, so that intimacy does not depend solely on moments of crisis.

## Main Task

Learn to remain connected to your partner precisely when instinct tells you to retreat—turning your natural need for space into an agreed pause rather than a silent escape.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- When did I last withdraw from my partner during a conflict instead of staying in the conversation?
- Do I share a living feeling with my partner, or only a finished analysis of the situation?

## Practical Plan

1. Agree with your partner on a specific phrase that signals a pause and a clear time for returning to the conversation.
2. The next time you feel the urge to retreat, try naming one feeling to your partner before you begin explaining it.
3. Once a week, share something from your inner world with your partner without waiting for a conflict or problem.
