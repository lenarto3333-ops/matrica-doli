# 10. Wheel of Fortune — Ancestral Tasks, Patterns, and Resources

## Brief Essence

The ancestral pattern of the tenth energy is often connected with sharp turns of fate—relocation, the loss and restoration of status, or sudden changes in circumstances the family could not control. The lineage may include ancestors whose lives were radically changed by war, revolution, exile, sudden wealth, or equally sudden poverty: people forced to begin again from nothing more than once, or those who learned to survive through an ability to adapt quickly to new conditions.

The ancestral task of this energy is to end the cycle of dependence on external turns of fate: to draw on the lineage's flexibility and resilience without inheriting its constant anxiety about change or, conversely, its habit of passively awaiting the next rescue from outside. The task is to restore the family's capacity to shape its own destiny actively rather than merely adapting to other people's decisions.

## Natural Strength

The ancestral resource of this energy is a capacity, accumulated over generations, to survive and adapt to radically new circumstances. The family may include people who managed to begin life again in a new place, a new country, or new conditions, and this ancestral resilience is passed down as the ability not to fear change but to see opportunities within it.

Another resource is a family tradition of practical ingenuity: merchants, travelers, and artisans who knew how to seize the right moment for a deal or decision and whose good fortune was created not by inheritance but by their own sense of timing. This legacy appears as a descendant's natural ability to notice opportunities and act quickly when needed.

One more resource is the lineage's ability to preserve optimism and hope even in the hardest circumstances. Ancestors who survived catastrophic losses without losing faith that life would improve again left their descendants an inner foundation that makes it possible to remain steady amid uncertainty—provided this foundation does not become a habit of planning nothing because “everything can change anyway.”

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, the ancestral resource of adaptability appears as a descendant's ability to be the family member who can find a way out of any difficult situation, preserving calm and practicality where others panic. The person becomes an example to the family that recovery is possible even after the harshest turn.

The ancestral task is fulfilled when a person consciously transforms inherited flexibility into stability—making long-term plans and passing on to their children not only stories about the changeability of fate, but practical skills for creating their own well-being rather than merely surviving after fate's blows.

The positive also appears as the ability to honor the family's tradition of resilience in the face of change without turning it into an ancestral fear of stability. The person can build a reliable, predictable life while remaining open to new opportunities when they are genuinely worthwhile.

Another sign of the positive is the ability to regard with respect the ancestors whose lives changed sharply against their will and consciously bring this family story to completion: transforming the memory of losses endured into gratitude for the resilience gained rather than constant expectation of another catastrophe.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

In the positive, a woman often becomes the person in the family who finds opportunity where others see only crisis—a practical, worldly-wise adviser whom relatives seek out in uncertain periods. She consciously passes on to her children and grandchildren not only a story of survival, but the ability to build stability from what life provides.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

In the positive, a man carries the ancestral tradition of resilience and adaptability as a foundation rather than as anxiety. He can be the person the family relies on during periods of change, preserving practical clarity where others lose their bearings. He consciously passes on to his sons or younger family members the ability to see opportunities in difficult circumstances without infecting them with a constant fear of instability.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, the ancestral pattern of instability repeats literally: the person feels a deep, often unspoken unease with any stability, as though constantly expecting the next turn to destroy everything, repeating the fear of an ancestor whose life truly was destroyed by someone else's will or by circumstances.

The ancestral task is blocked when inherited flexibility becomes an inability to build anything lasting: the family lives from crisis to crisis without investing in the future because “there is no point in planning anyway,” and this model is passed to children as the only familiar way of living.

Another sign of the negative is an ancestral shadow of gambling and recklessness. The family may have developed a pattern of risky, ill-considered decisions intended to improve its situation quickly, repeatedly leading to new losses, and a descendant may unconsciously continue this pattern even while consciously condemning it.

One more negative expression is an ancestral pattern of passively awaiting rescue: the story of an ancestor saved by a lucky break or someone else's help comes alive in a descendant as a habit of waiting for someone or something outside to resolve financial or life difficulties instead of taking the initiative.

## What Can Shift the Energy into the Negative

The strongest trigger is a family situation involving instability or a sharp change in circumstances that repeats an old ancestral pattern of losing control. A second trigger is prolonged, enforced stability with no room for movement or development, which a family with this pattern experiences as stagnation opposed to its inherited need for renewal. A third is a financial or life crisis that activates the inherited fear that if the wheel turns the wrong way, everything will once again have to begin from nothing.

## How to Return the Energy to the Positive

The first step is to understand whose fate lies behind the family's fear of change or, conversely, its habit of constant instability: if possible, learn the family history and identify a particular ancestor or story connected with a sudden turn of fate. The second is symbolically or literally to “take back” that ancestor's valuable resource—their adaptability, resilience, and ability to start again—without taking on their anxiety and fear as well.

The third step is to practice building long-term plans consciously despite the family's experience of instability, reminding yourself each time that present circumstances offer more opportunities for control than the ancestor had in the past. The fourth is to notice moments of inherited recklessness or, conversely, passive waiting for rescue, and consciously choose measured, active participation in your own life.

It is also helpful to separate ancestral flexibility as a resource from ancestral anxiety as a burden: the first helps you adapt, while the second exhausts you in advance. Regular planning combined with openness to new opportunities helps break the cycle of family instability without depriving the lineage of its natural resilience.

## Main Task

The main ancestral task of this energy is to end the cycle of dependence on external turns of fate: take what is valuable and enduring in the family's capacity to survive and adapt, and transform it into the conscious, active creation of stable well-being for future generations instead of merely reacting to each new crisis again and again.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Is there a story in my family of a sharp turn of fate—loss, exile, sudden poverty, or sudden wealth?
- What part of my family's resource of adaptability do I want to preserve, and what do I consciously choose not to repeat?
- Do I make long-term plans, or do I live from crisis to crisis out of ancestral habit?
- Where does an ancestral pattern of passively awaiting rescue from outside appear in my life?
- Does my family have a tendency toward ill-considered risk as an attempt to fix everything quickly?
- To what extent is my anxiety about stability or, conversely, about change based on my own experience, and to what extent is it inherited fear?
- What would change in my family if I consciously ended the cycle of ancestral instability?

## Practical Plan

1. Learn or recall the story of a relative whose fate changed sharply against their will, and write down what resilience or adaptability they showed.
2. Make one long-term plan for yourself or your family despite the familiar ancestral anxiety that “you shouldn't tempt fate.”
3. Notice one instance of inherited recklessness or passive waiting for rescue, and consciously choose a measured, active response.
4. Pay attention to a moment of anxiety about stability or, conversely, about change, and explore whether it comes from your own experience or from ancestral fear.
5. Plan a regular financial or life-planning practice that also leaves room for new opportunities.
6. Write briefly which ancestral resilience or flexibility you want to preserve and carry forward to your children or successors.
