# 9. The Hermit — Ancestral Tasks, Patterns, and Resources

## Brief Essence

The ancestral pattern of the ninth energy is often connected with solitude—chosen or imposed—and with wisdom gained at the high price of isolation. The family line may include ancestors who held knowledge, a craft, or a profound understanding of the world but had neither the means nor the permission to pass it on openly: sages who were misunderstood, people forced to remain silent about important matters, or those who withdrew from family and community because there was no place there for their depth.

The ancestral task of this energy is to complete the cycle of solitary, unshared wisdom: to draw on the resource of the family line’s deep knowledge without also inheriting the fear of rejection for being different. The task is to restore the family’s ability to share what it has accumulated instead of hiding its most valuable gifts in silence again and again.

## Natural Strength

The ancestral resource of this energy is a capacity, built over generations, to think independently, resist group pressure, and preserve inner integrity even when the surrounding world demands conformity. The family may have included people who went against the current for the sake of truth and chose a profession or way of thinking that was inconvenient for their time. Their resilience is passed down as the ability not to fear being different.

Another resource is a family tradition of approaching work deeply and thoughtfully: artisans who refined their craft for years, scholars, healers, and family advisers whose knowledge was valued for its care and thoroughness rather than speed. This legacy appears as a descendant’s natural ability to immerse themselves in a subject seriously and reach its essence instead of settling for superficial knowledge.

There is also the family’s ability to survive and retain dignity through periods of forced isolation: displacement, loss of community, or separation from a familiar environment. Ancestors who endured loneliness without breaking left their descendants an inner foundation that makes it possible to feel whole without constant outside support—provided that this foundation does not become a habit of shutting out living relationships.

## Expression in the Positive

In the positive, the ancestral resource of deep thought and independence appears in a descendant’s ability to see the heart of a family problem where others see only symptoms, and to offer advice grounded in genuine understanding rather than emotional reaction. They become the person relatives turn to with difficult, tangled questions, trusting their capacity for impartial thought.

The ancestral task is fulfilled when a person consciously passes the family’s accumulated knowledge, experience, or wisdom to children, younger relatives, or the wider community rather than keeping it to themselves for fear of being misunderstood. They feel a deep connection with ancestors who also valued silence and depth and use that bond as a source of strength, not as an excuse for detachment.

The positive also means honoring the family tradition of independent thought without turning it into an inherited prejudice against closeness and trust. A person can be independent while remaining deeply rooted in family relationships, staying connected and sharing their thoughts rather than standing apart behind the belief that “they will not understand me anyway.”

Another sign is treating with respect those ancestors whose wisdom or difference went unrecognized in their lifetime and consciously bringing their story to completion: restoring the value of deep knowledge in the family and speaking about it aloud rather than carrying it silently as an isolated personal trait.

A person also learns to distinguish the inherited gift of independence from the inherited pattern of avoidance: they choose solitude as a resource for thought, not as the only familiar defense against family conflict or misunderstanding.

### Expression in Women in the Positive

In the positive, a woman often becomes the keeper of deep family knowledge or wisdom—the person others approach for counsel on important family matters, and who can pass this knowledge on in a way that inspires the younger generation’s trust rather than intimidating them. She consciously reconnects her own need for quiet with a warm presence in the family, sacrificing neither.

### Expression in Men in the Positive

In the positive, a man carries the family tradition of independent, thorough thought as a foundation rather than a wall. He can be someone the family relies on for difficult decisions while maintaining warm, living contact with relatives. He consciously teaches his sons or younger family members the value of approaching things deeply without demanding that they share his need for solitude.

## Expression in the Negative

In the negative, the ancestral pattern of loneliness repeats literally: a person feels a deep, often unspoken pull to keep apart from the family, retracing the path of an ancestor whose wisdom or difference was once rejected. This may appear as difficulty maintaining warm contact with relatives and a habit of withholding what matters precisely where openness is needed most.

The ancestral task is blocked when the family’s accumulated wisdom—practical, spiritual, or professional—is not passed on because its bearer believes no one needs it or will value it properly. Valuable knowledge dies with the person instead of becoming the next generation’s inheritance.

Another sign is the ancestral shadow of the expert’s pride: the family may have developed a pattern in which its “intelligent” or “deep” members quietly disdain relatives who are simpler or less reflective, creating an invisible hierarchy of superiority that destroys warmth. A descendant may unconsciously continue this pattern even while consciously condemning it.

There may also be a pattern of imposed rather than chosen solitude. The story of an ancestor exiled, abandoned, or isolated by the community returns in the descendant as an irrational fear of intimacy, suspicion of groups, and a sense that belonging anywhere is dangerous or impossible.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger is a family situation in which depth, seriousness, or the need for solitude is mocked or devalued, repeating the old pattern of misunderstanding. A second is being forced into a noisy, superficial family environment with no right to boundaries or quiet. A third is a family conflict or rupture that activates the inherited fear: if I reveal my true depth, I will be rejected just as my ancestor once was.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

First, recognize whose loneliness or unshared wisdom may lie behind your own pull to stand apart. If possible, learn the family history and identify an ancestor or story associated with withdrawal or devalued depth. Then symbolically or literally reclaim that ancestor’s valuable resource—their knowledge, resilience, and independent thought—without taking on their loneliness and pain of being misunderstood.

Consciously practice passing knowledge on within the family: tell younger people what you know and understand deeply, even if doing so feels unfamiliar or vulnerable. Notice moments of inherited intellectual pride and deliberately return to warm, equal contact with relatives whose lives are simpler or noisier than your own.

It is also useful to separate solitude as a resource from isolation as a defense: the first nourishes, the second exhausts. Maintaining regular, even if infrequent, warm contact with family—a call, letter, or shared event—helps break the cycle of ancestral detachment without requiring you to change your nature.

## Main Task

The central ancestral task of this energy is to complete the cycle of solitary, unshared wisdom: to take what is valuable and profound in the family’s keeping and bring it into the light—to pass it on to children, the community, and those who come after instead of repeatedly hiding what matters most in silence and detachment.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- Is there someone in my family line whose wisdom, difference, or depth was not accepted or understood?
- What part of my family’s independent thinking do I want to preserve, and what do I consciously choose not to repeat?
- Do I pass my knowledge and experience on to children or younger relatives, or keep it entirely to myself?
- Where in my family relationships do I choose silence when openness could be healing?
- Do I feel a quiet superiority toward relatives who are simpler or less reflective?
- How much of my need for solitude is my own choice, and how much is an inherited fear of closeness?
- What would change in my family if I consciously brought the cycle of ancestral detachment to an end?

## Practical Plan

1. Learn or recall the story of a relative whose wisdom, depth, or difference went unrecognized, and write down what was valuable about them.
2. Choose one piece of knowledge or experience you possess and intentionally share it with a younger relative this month.
3. Identify one area of family silence—a subject never openly discussed—and take one small step toward openness.
4. Notice one moment of quiet condescension toward simpler relatives and consciously choose warm contact over distance.
5. Schedule regular, even brief, contact with family that does not depend on your mood or need for solitude.
6. Write down the particular resilience or wisdom from your family line that you want to preserve and pass on to your children or successors.
