# 16. The Tower — Personal, Social, and Spiritual Purpose

## Brief Essence

The personal purpose of this energy is to learn to go through the inevitable breakdowns of life not as a victim of circumstances, but as a person who consciously lets fall what has long been held on to an illusion, and builds something more real in the vacated place. In younger years, this often manifests as a series of sharp, painful transitions—a breakup, a move, a plan collapse—in adulthood, as the ability to initiate the necessary changes on your own without waiting for life to force it, and in later life, it grows into the wisdom that every big fall in life was actually clearing a place for a more accurate version of yourself.

The social purpose is manifested through the role of a person who is not afraid to say out loud that the old structure - the relationship, the system, the common cause - has exhausted itself and keeps others stagnant for the sake of the illusion of stability. Spiritual purpose leads through one's own experience of profound upheaval—when one loses what seemed to be an unbreakable foundation along the way, and it is in this loss, not in spite of it, that one finds true spiritual awakening and strength, which one ultimately shares with others who are going through their own crises.

## Natural Strength

The personal resource of this energy is a rare ability not to cling to a form that has outlived itself: a person intuitively feels the moment when a structure - a profession, a relationship, a belief - has become a cell, not a support, and it is this sensitivity that allows him to let go of the old before it becomes a painful compulsion.

A social resource is the ability to speak the truth at a time when others prefer to remain silent for the sake of feigned peace: a person sees hidden cracks in a system, community or project long before they become obvious, and has the courage to say it out loud, even when such honesty causes resistance.

A spiritual resource is the ability to transform through a direct encounter with loss: a person does not avoid a crisis, but goes through it completely, and it is this experience that gives him the depth of understanding that the real basis of life is not external stability, but internal stability, which does not depend on any specific structure.

## Expression in the Positive

At a younger age, personal purpose is revealed through the ability to go through early crises and breakdowns with dignity - parental divorce, changing schools, losing an important relationship - without being stuck in the role of a victim, but gradually learning to see each such experience as material for personal growth.

Social purpose manifests itself as the role of a person who knows how to initiate necessary, albeit painful, changes in a group or community: she is the first to name what everyone feels but is afraid to say - that the old structure no longer serves - and it is this honesty, despite resistance, that ultimately opens the way to a healthier association.

Spiritual purpose in the positive is manifested as the ability to consciously accompany others through their crises, without trying to soften or accelerate the natural process of destruction and reconstruction: a person who himself has gone through a deep shock and found awakening in it becomes a reliable guide for those who are now at the darkest point of their own brokenness.

Another positive expression is the ability to see hidden opportunity where others see only disaster: a person naturally recognizes what new growth lies behind loss or destruction, and shares this vision with those who cannot yet see it for themselves.

A common feature of realized purpose is inner freedom from the fear of losing structure: a person knows from his own experience that even the complete destruction of external support does not destroy him, and this confidence gives him the rare courage to live and act honestly, without clinging to illusions for the sake of false peace.

### Expression in Women (Positive)

In a woman, this purpose often manifests as the ability to be the first to dare to break an old mold—a relationship, a role, an expectation—when she feels it no longer fits the truth of her life, and to do so with respect for herself and others, not through a destructive outburst.

As she gets older, she becomes more confident accompanying other women through their own crises and transitions, sharing the experience that after the most painful fall, it is indeed possible to rebuild a life that feels more honest and stronger than before.

### Expression in Men (Positive)

In a man, this purpose manifests itself as the ability to decisively and consciously dismantle what no longer serves - an outdated career, a toxic structure, an illusory plan - without waiting for circumstances to make it more painful and destructive than himself.

Over time, this ability often turns into the recognized role of a person who is turned to precisely in moments of crisis or breakdown, because he knows how to keep calm and clarity where others panic, based on his own experience that the structure can always be rebuilt anew.

## Expression in the Negative

When the purpose is not realized, the destructive potential of the person turns against himself and against others: instead of consciously letting go of the unsustainable structure, he clings to the illusion until the last, and when the collapse does occur, he reacts with an explosion of anger, accusations or breaking off the relationship in the most destructive way possible.

In a social role, the negative manifests itself as a chronic destroyer: a person either constantly provokes conflicts and ruptures where a gradual, softer change would be possible, or, on the contrary, is so afraid of any upheaval that he blocks the necessary changes in himself and the environment, until the crisis explodes by itself, destroying much more than could be lost during conscious restructuring.

Another manifestation of the negative is being stuck in the role of the eternal victim of a disaster: a person identifies himself so much with the experienced shock that he does not allow himself to move on, repeats the same story of loss over and over again, and this unassimilated trauma begins to determine each new decision with fear, not with wisdom.

In the deeper negative, chronic hyperarousal and readiness for attack appear: a person lives in constant expectation of the next blow, reacts sharply even to minor challenges, and this accumulated internal aggression periodically spills out on loved ones in the form of outbursts, which themselves become a new trauma - already for others.

## What Can Push You Into the Negative

The strongest trigger is long-term retention of an unsustainable structure due to fear of the unknown, when a person feels the approach of collapse, but knowingly or unknowingly postpones the necessary change until it explodes on its own, in the most destructive way. The second trigger is the experience of betrayal or a sharp, unfair blow from someone you trust, which leaves behind distrust and a willingness to attack first, so as not to be caught off guard again. The third is accumulated, unexpressed anger or pain from past upheavals, which erupts disproportionately at the slightest new stress.

## How to Return Energy to the Positive

The first step is to honestly identify what structure in your own life—relationships, work, beliefs—has long been based on an illusion rather than a real foundation, and to name it directly, without procrastination. The second step is, where the necessary change is ripe, to initiate it consciously and gradually while it is still manageable, rather than waiting until circumstances make it abrupt and painful.

The third step is to consciously work with accumulated anxiety or anger from past upheavals so that they don't determine responses to current challenges: therapy, body practices, or regular talking through with someone you trust can help. The fourth step is to seek professional support rather than relying on your own endurance if it's a repetitive strain injury or overexertion.

It is also useful to periodically ask yourself which of your structures you are holding on to out of fear and which ones are held out of real need, and whether it is time to let something fall consciously before it falls on its own.

## Main Task

The main task of this assignment is to learn to consciously destroy what no longer serves the truth of one's life, and to use every shock, external or initiated by the person himself, as a path to a deeper awakening, and not as an excuse for despair or aggression. Realized purpose means that a person is no longer afraid of falling, because he knows from his own experience: after it, you can always build something stronger and more honest.

## Questions for Self-Reflection

- What structure in my life do I keep out of fear and not out of real need?
- Am I not reacting to challenges more sharply than the situation requires, due to accumulated anxiety from the past?
- What lesson from my biggest shock have I still not fully learned?

## Practical Plan

1. Name one structure in your life that is based on an illusion, and describe the first step to consciously changing it.
2. Think back to the last time you reacted disproportionately to a challenge, and think about where that reaction actually came from.
3. Pick one necessary but delayed change and take the first conscious step toward it this week while it's still manageable.
4. If you notice chronic anxiety or readiness to attack, take the first concrete step to professional support.
5. Find someone who is currently going through their own crisis and offer them support based on your own experience of overcoming shock.
6. Once a month, conduct a personal "audit of structures" - review what in your life is held on a real basis, and what has been waiting for a long time to be consciously released.
