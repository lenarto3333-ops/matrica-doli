# 12. The Hanged Man — Point of Inner Comfort

## Understanding the Point of Inner Comfort

The point of inner comfort describes the state in which it is easier to recover, make decisions, and feel that you are living in harmony with yourself. It is neither a demand to remain in one mood at all times nor an assessment of character. Rather, it offers clues about the conditions that support inner balance and the signs that your familiar way of living has begun to deplete you.

For the twelfth energy, comfort is connected with the right to stop and view a situation differently without hurrying to resolve it at once. It is important to have permission for a pause that may look like inactivity from the outside, but is actually the space in which new understanding is born. This is not a need to remain suspended between decisions forever, but a deeper trust that sometimes the best movement is to stop moving temporarily, allowing your perspective to shift and reveal what remained invisible at your usual pace.

## What Fills You with Energy (12)

Nothing restores you more than permission to pause without explanation or guilt—a moment when you can say, “I simply need to sit with this,” and have that need respected rather than being pressured to decide immediately or prove your productivity. When slowing down requires no justification, tension subsides on its own.

A change in perspective is also restorative: viewing a familiar situation from an unusual side, listening to an opinion that challenges your assumptions, or trying an unconventional approach without fearing that it will look strange or wrong. The twelfth energy comes alive when it is not required to conform immediately to the obvious solution.

An environment where slowing down is not punished is important—where no one presses you with “Well, have you decided?” and silence and reflection are treated as part of the process rather than laziness or weak will. Even one person who can wait without devaluing your pause can significantly relieve the pressure.

The experience of releasing control is replenishing as well: not keeping everything under personal supervision, trusting the course of events, and allowing a situation to “mature” instead of forcing the outcome through willpower. This is not passivity for its own sake, but recognition that some answers simply are not ready yet—and that this is normal.

Space for nonlinear thought is another resource: a metaphor, image, symbol, creative activity, or meditative practice in which thought can move in circles rather than a straight line and discover unexpected connections. This is often where understanding emerges that cannot be found while chasing a quick conclusion.

Physical calm matters too—the ability to lie down, slow your breathing, and let the body remain still without demanding that it rush somewhere immediately. When the body is granted a pause, the mind also stops driving itself and can finally hear its own thoughts through to the end.

## Resourceful State of the Energy

In a resourceful state, a person knows how to stop consciously without treating it as failure or wasted time. They calmly endure uncertainty while an answer is not yet ready and do not panic because “nothing has been decided.” For them, the pause is not emptiness, but part of the process.

A rare ability emerges to see the situation from an angle unavailable to those in a hurry. The resourceful Hanged Man offers solutions that initially seem strange, then prove the most precise precisely because they arose from calm observation rather than pressure. Others notice an unshakable clarity in this person: they do not become frantic when everyone else does.

In this state, a person is not afraid to be “between”—between decisions, stages, or roles—because they trust that the interval also has meaning. Their calm is contagious: others nearby permit themselves to slow down too, and the decisions eventually made rest on clarity rather than fear of error.

## Signs of Accumulated Tension

The first sign is often becoming stuck while waiting for the “right moment.” Step after step is postponed under the belief that it is not yet time, although fear of mistakes or change is actually hiding beneath the delay. The pause meant to bring clarity becomes endless procrastination, and the longer it lasts, the harder it is to leave.

The second sign is an inner sense of suspension and powerlessness, as though life were passing while the person “waits for the right opportunity.” This is no longer a conscious pause but paralyzing passivity: movement is frightening, remaining still is exhausting, and what appears calm outside conceals growing anxiety within.

Another signal is irritation or confusion when comparing yourself with people who act quickly and decisively. Hidden guilt about your slower pace appears, along with a desire to justify the pause, although that same pause recently felt like a natural need rather than a weakness.

In relationships, accumulated tension often appears as silent postponement of an important conversation. You know something must be said or decided but delay repeatedly, hoping the “right moment” will arrive on its own. If this persists and is accompanied by lasting sadness or anxiety that does not ease, consider speaking with an appropriate professional.

## How to Restore Your Strength

1. Allow yourself one conscious pause each day in which nothing needs to be decided or planned.
2. Write down the decision you are postponing and ask honestly: is this a pause for clarity or an escape from the fear of making a mistake?
3. Try viewing a stalled situation through someone else’s eyes, or imagine how it might look a year from now.
4. Identify one small step you can take now, even before complete clarity arrives.
5. Create space for nonlinear thought through drawing, walking, music, or any activity without the immediate goal of finding an answer.
6. Give your body physical rest: lie down, slow your breathing, and stop pushing yourself to hurry somewhere.
7. Notice when you compare yourself with faster-moving people, and remind yourself that speed is not a measure of whether a decision is right.
8. Set a specific deadline for the anticipated “right moment,” and reassess honestly when it arrives.
9. Talk with someone who knows how to wait without devaluing your pause, and share what is holding you back.
10. If waiting has become a permanent trap, consider speaking with a psychologist who can help distinguish a maturing pause from defensive procrastination.

## Summary: A Gradual Return to Balance

Balance in the twelfth energy returns neither when a person remains forever paused nor when they force themselves to act against an inner sense that it is too soon. It returns when the pause once again becomes a conscious choice rather than a place where fear has trapped them. The ability to see from an unusual angle remains valuable, while the illusion disappears that endless waiting alone will bring clarity closer.

You do not have to choose between calm and action. Mature comfort unites them: the pause provides a new perspective, and action embodies it in life before the moment passes. Return to balance in small steps—through one conscious stop, one modest action instead of another wait, and trust that the right moment often begins not with perfect circumstances, but with your decision to finally move.
